\begin{abstract}
Training-free chain-of-thought early-stopping methods decide whether to halt generation by checking a reasoning trajectory's current answer, usually together with its token confidence, against a threshold. This assumes the current observable state, not the full history that produced it, already carries what the decision needs. We test this with a matched-pair natural-branching design on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B and MATH-500: we identify pairs of reasoning prefixes from the same problem but different trajectories that state the same answer at the matched checkpoint, then let each side continue naturally many times and compare their future-answer distributions. \textbf{Across 66 matched pairs and 17 distinct problems, observable state is sufficient.} 14 of 17 problems converge exactly in every sampled pair regardless of history, and every pair genuinely matched on the full observable state, confidence and entropy included, shows zero divergence without exception.

Three problems initially appeared to be exceptions, driving a small but statistically significant pooled effect (problem-clustered permutation test, $p=0.0005$, against a null built from within-pair resplitting). We examined each directly rather than report the aggregate test at face value, because the effect it detects is substantively small: 0.62\% of the metric's bounded range, and smaller than the typical sampling variation inside a single matched pair. None of the three exceptions holds up. One is a known answer-normalization artifact. A second tracks a sampling-configuration switch (top-$p=0.95$ versus a default setting) rather than any difference in matched-state history. For the third, we ran a properly-powered follow-up that instead refuted the mechanism we had proposed to explain it (divergence persisted even with confidence matched within tolerance), and a direct check of the underlying data showed the four pairs we had built the finding on were not four independent observations: they came from only four distinct prefixes, and one prefix's own branch sets, resampled against themselves with no difference in history whatsoever, spanned the entire error-rate range the proposed mechanism was built to explain. We report both this finding and the two-round process of testing it directly, because a small aggregate effect concentrated in a handful of reused prefixes is exactly the kind of result a careful design should catch before publishing it as a positive claim, and this one nearly was not caught. Two further checks, run against the null itself rather than for it, support the conclusion: answer normalization does not manufacture the zero-divergence majority (56 of 66 pairs are zero on raw, un-normalized strings), and the 1500-token branch budget does not suppress divergence globally (empty answers are concentrated entirely in the two problems we identified, zero elsewhere across 2,140 branches). A stopping rule built on a confidence gate in addition to answer stability (Sufficiency-Gated Early Stopping) does not beat the simplest fixed-fraction baseline on either accuracy or compute, exactly what this result predicts. All corrections to our own analysis, including two proposed mechanisms we tested and withdrew and one implementation error in our own audit design, are documented in full in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixa} rather than narrated through the results themselves.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Chain-of-thought reasoning is expensive, and a growing body of work tries to cut that cost by stopping generation as soon as a model has effectively decided its answer, without any additional training. Our own earlier pilot on this model illustrates why the idea is attractive: MATH-500 accuracy is \emph{non-monotonic} in trace length. Truncating generation to 75\% of its natural length measured 7.5 points \emph{higher} accuracy than letting the model run to completion. Reasoning models sometimes talk themselves out of answers they already had. If a stopping rule can reliably tell when that point has been reached, it saves both compute and accuracy.

Every training-free method for making that call, whether it compares current answer stability, token confidence, predictive entropy, or some combination of the three against a threshold, makes the same implicit assumption. This \textbf{current observable state} is treated as what the decision should be based on, because it already captures everything relevant about where the trajectory is headed. Two trajectories that currently look identical to the stopping rule are treated as interchangeable from that point forward. This assumption is never stated as a hypothesis, let alone tested. It is simply the feature space these methods are built on.

We ask this directly, as a testable hypothesis rather than an assumption: \textbf{does a reasoning trajectory's current observable state, its answer, confidence, and entropy, predict its eventual answer as well as its full history would, or is history-dependent divergence detectable even after two trajectories agree on current state?} We call the latter possibility \emph{path dependence}, and we test for it with a matched-pair natural-branching design: prefixes from different trajectories on the same problem, matched on current observable state, branched forward naturally many times, with their future-answer distributions compared against a matched permutation null. This is a test of the stronger claim that early-stopping methods only need implicitly (Section~\ref{sec:method-hyp}): no deployed method requires strict statistical sufficiency, only that current state be a useful decision feature, and a sufficiency result, if it holds, makes the weaker claim safe automatically.

\textbf{Our finding is that it holds.} Across 66 matched pairs spanning 17 distinct MATH-500 problems, 56 pairs produce \emph{exactly} identical answer distributions regardless of history, the modal outcome by a wide margin, and every pair that happens to also match on confidence and entropy (7 pairs, not by design, but because both sides had already fully committed to an answer) shows zero divergence without a single exception. A problem-clustered permutation test finds a small but statistically significant departure from the null in the pooled data (\emph{p} = 0.0005), concentrated in 3 of the 17 problems. Rather than report this aggregate result at face value, we checked what it was built on, motivated by how small the effect is in absolute terms: 0.0062 mean excess divergence against a metric bounded at 1, smaller than the typical sampling noise inside a single matched pair (0.0098). \textbf{Every one of the three apparent exceptions has a specific, disposable explanation.} One is a known answer-normalization artifact. A second tracks a generation-configuration switch (top-\emph{p} = 0.95 versus a default setting), which we confirm with a targeted audit rerun at the correct configuration. For the third, and most striking on first inspection, we ran a direct follow-up that refuted our own proposed mechanism (confidence-matched pairs still diverged) and then found the underlying finding itself rested on pseudoreplication: the "four independent pairs" it was built on came from only four distinct prefixes, and one prefix's own branch sets, resampled against themselves with zero difference in history, spanned the entire effect size the mechanism was built to explain.

We also correct a separate, more basic error from an earlier draft of this paper: we had argued the continuous state-distance regression's \emph{intercept} showed divergence closer to significance than a null slope suggested (0.0214, \emph{p} = 0.086). This was not a defensible reading. Seven of our 45 regression pairs sit at exactly zero state-distance, a direct measurement, not a fitted extrapolation, and every one of them shows zero divergence. The fitted intercept was pulled upward by points roughly half a state-unit away, it is geometry, not evidence. We report the direct measurement instead of the fitted line.

Two further checks, run against the null rather than for it, support the same conclusion. Checking all 66 pairs for normalizer-manufactured zeros (the opposite of the one artifact we found) turned up none: 56 of 66 pairs are zero on raw, un-normalized strings before any normalization runs. Checking whether the 1500-token branch cap suppresses divergence globally, not just in the two problems a targeted audit examined, showed empties concentrated entirely in those two problems, zero across 2,140 branches everywhere else. Full detail on every correction and every audit, including one implementation error in our own audit design and the two mechanisms we proposed and then withdrew, is in Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixa} rather than threaded through the results below.

\textbf{Contributions:}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A matched-pair natural-branching test of whether observable state is sufficient for chain-of-thought early stopping, at 66 pairs across 17 MATH-500 problems on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, finding sufficiency holds, including for the specific cases that initially looked like exceptions.
  \item A methodological result we regard as at least as valuable as the headline finding: two mechanisms proposed to explain an apparent minority-case exception were tested directly rather than reported at face value, and a small, statistically significant aggregate effect was traced to pseudoreplication (one resampled prefix accounting for the entire effect size a three-pair mechanism was built to explain).
  \item Two real statistical corrections along the way, reported with the reasoning behind each: a degenerate-\emph{p}-value failure mode in Fisher's method that initially concealed a small real effect in the raw data (fixed via a problem-clustered permutation test), and a withdrawal of an earlier claim about the state-distance regression's intercept once checked against pairs measured directly at zero distance.
  \item Two stress tests run specifically against our own conclusion, one on measurement (does answer normalization manufacture false convergence), one on design (does the branch token budget suppress divergence globally), both of which support the null rather than complicate it.
  \item A direct test of the practical implication: a stopping rule built on confidence in addition to answer stability (Sufficiency-Gated Early Stopping) does not outperform the simplest fixed-fraction baseline, exactly what this paper's finding predicts.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedwork}

\paragraph{Training-free early-stopping for chain-of-thought reasoning.} A growing line of work detects when a reasoning trajectory has effectively decided its answer and halts generation early, without any additional training. REFRAIN \citep{refrain2026} segments traces on the same blank-line convention we use and benchmarks a broad family of such signals (No-thinking, DEER, HALT-CoT, CoT-Valve, Deepconf) against each other. Statistical Early Stopping for Reasoning Models \citep{statisticalearlystopping2026} frames the problem statistically and reports recovering roughly 82 to 85\% of an oracle stopping policy's power. ThinkBrake \citep{thinkbrake2026} constructs its oracle via forced termination and shows an 8\% accuracy gain and a 72\% token reduction against it. \textbf{All of these methods, and the broader family they represent, share the same implicit assumption this paper tests directly}, that the trajectory's current observable state is what the stopping decision should, and safely can, be based on. None of them, to our knowledge, tests whether that state is a sufficient statistic for the trajectory's eventual answer, as opposed to simply using it as the stopping feature.

\paragraph{Forced extraction and the detection-extraction gap.} The closest prior work to our own mechanism is \emph{The Detection-Extraction Gap: Models Know the Answer Before They Can Say It} \citep{detectionextractiongap2026}, which compares a model's free, natural continuation against a forced-extraction completion \textbf{at the same prefix}, using the identical priming suffix wording we independently arrived at (\texttt{"...the final answer is \textbackslash boxed\{"}). They report this detection-extraction gap decaying from around 54 percentage points early in a trajectory to under 5 points as the trajectory approaches its own natural stopping point, and mechanistically attribute the vanishing gap to the forced suffix becoming an increasingly natural continuation as the trajectory concludes. This result is why we do \textbf{not} frame our contribution as forced extraction differing from natural continuation. That question, at a single fixed prefix, is essentially answered by their work. \textbf{Our design is different in kind, not degree.} Rather than comparing forced against natural continuation at one prefix, we compare \emph{natural} continuation across \emph{two different prefixes} that already agree on observable state. The question is not whether forcing an answer early changes it, but whether two trajectories that currently look identical to an early-stopping method can still diverge naturally, because of where they came from, not how they are asked to conclude. Their evaluation also does not include DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, the model family used throughout this paper.

\paragraph{Counterfactual resampling within a single trajectory.} Two recent lines of work resample from intermediate points of a generation to measure how much a specific token or sentence matters. \citet{forkingpaths2024} resample at every token position within one trajectory and identify "forking tokens", points where resampling produces a markedly different completion, sometimes as innocuous as a single punctuation mark. \citet{thoughtanchors2025} resample at sentence boundaries within one reasoning trace and measure each sentence's counterfactual importance to the final answer, calling influential sentences "thought anchors". Both methods intervene at one point in a single trajectory and ask how much that one point matters. Our design asks a different question: we take \emph{two independently generated trajectories} that have already reached the same observable state, and ask whether their futures still diverge. This is not a within-trajectory importance measurement, it is a cross-trajectory sufficiency test, whether the observable summary two trajectories currently share is enough to predict that they will continue to agree, or whether unshared history still leaks through. A forking-token or thought-anchor analysis could in principle explain \emph{why} two matched trajectories in our design diverge, by locating the point in each one's separate history where they committed to different downstream behavior, but neither method as published tests the matched-pair sufficiency question directly.

\paragraph{Representation and routing-level state.} RAD \citep{rad2026} ("Does the Same Token Mean the Same State?") studies whether identical tokens correspond to identical hidden states or mixture-of-experts routing decisions, a different, lower-level notion of state than the observable $\phi(p)$ (answer, confidence, entropy) this paper operates on, and explicitly out of our scope. The Beginning Lock-in Effect and PPPO line of work \citep{pppo2025} studies one-directional causal influence of early tokens on later generation, which is related in spirit but not a matched-state counterfactual design. It does not ask whether two trajectories at the \emph{same} current state can still diverge, only whether early choices causally constrain later ones along a single trajectory.

\paragraph{Censoring and selection bias in early-stopping evaluation.} A separate methodological concern, orthogonal to the question this paper asks, is that fixed generation budgets used to construct early-stopping oracles and evaluation sets can systematically exclude the hardest, longest-reasoning problems from the analysis. The Coupling Tax \citep{couplingtax2026} develops Kaplan-Meier and censoring-aware machinery for a related but distinct question, whether to invoke visible chain-of-thought at all under a shared token budget. We do not apply this machinery here, but note it as a relevant tool for auditing the broader early-stopping oracle-construction literature, which is not yet known to account for it.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Hypothesis and unit of analysis}
\label{sec:method-hyp}

Every training-free chain-of-thought early-stopping method decides whether to stop based only on a reasoning trajectory's \emph{current observable state} $\phi(p)$, typically some combination of its current best-guess answer, token-level confidence, and predictive entropy. This implicitly assumes $\phi(p)$ is a \textbf{sufficient statistic} for the trajectory's eventual answer. Two trajectories that currently agree on $\phi(p)$ should, in expectation, go on to agree on their final answer too, regardless of how they arrived at that state.

We test this directly.

\textbf{H1 (path dependence):} two prefixes from different reasoning trajectories on the same problem, matched to the same $\phi(p) = (\text{answer}, \text{confidence}, \text{entropy})$ at the same normalized depth, can still diverge in their future-answer distribution by more than sampling noise predicts.

\textbf{H0 (null):} matched prefixes' future-answer distributions differ only by sampling noise. $\phi(p)$ is sufficient, and current-state-only early-stopping is not leaving predictive information on the table by ignoring history.

The unit of analysis is one \textbf{matched pair}: two prefixes, from different trajectories on the same problem, whose observable states match, each branched forward $N$ times. We compute a \textbf{Path Dependence Index (PDI)}, the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the two branch sets' answer distributions, and test it against a permutation null built from that pair's own pooled data.

Concretely, for future-answer distributions $P_A$ and $P_B$ over the two sides' branched outcomes, with mixture $M = \tfrac{1}{2}(P_A + P_B)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pdi}
\text{PDI}(A,B) \;=\; \text{JS}(P_A \,\|\, P_B) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_A \,\|\, M) \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_B \,\|\, M),
\end{equation}
computed with $\log_2$, so that $0 \le \text{PDI} \le 1$. As pre-registered, pair-level $p$-values were combined via Fisher's method within and across strata. Section~\ref{sec:results-primary} reports why this choice broke down on our data and the corrected aggregation we replaced it with (Section~\ref{sec:method-correction}).

\textbf{A note on what H1 does and does not claim, worth stating before any results.} No deployed early-stopping method actually assumes $\phi(p)$ is a sufficient statistic in the strict sense H1 tests. These methods assume $\phi(p)$ is a useful decision feature under a loss, that stopping when $\phi(p)$ crosses a threshold costs less in accuracy than it saves in compute. A feature can fail strict statistical sufficiency and still be an excellent stopping trigger, and the reverse can also hold. We test the stronger claim anyway, for a practical reason: sufficiency is the property that, if true, makes the weaker decision-feature claim safe \emph{a fortiori}, a stopping rule built on a genuinely sufficient statistic cannot be leaving predictive information on the table by construction. If H1 held (if history-dependent divergence were real and detectable), we would owe a further, decision-theoretic step this design does not provide: translating excess divergence into accuracy lost per unit of over-eager stopping. We do not owe that step for a null result, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports one.

\subsection{Trajectory generation and $\phi(p)$ scoring}
\label{sec:method-traj}

For each of 18 problems, we sample $K=5$ independent reasoning trajectories (temperature 0.6, top-$p$ 0.95, up to 2000 to 3000 new tokens, see Section~\ref{sec:lim-samplesize} for the one problem-specific exception) from DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B. Each trajectory is segmented into reasoning steps on blank-line boundaries, a convention validated in the project's original pilot as reliable (0\% of traces had fewer than 3 steps against a 30\% tolerance).

At every step boundary, we compute $\phi(p)$ for that prefix via \textbf{forced extraction}. We append the priming suffix \texttt{"\textbackslash nTherefore, the final answer is \textbackslash boxed\{"} and generate up to 64 more tokens. The resulting completion gives the answer, via brace-matched extraction of the boxed content, and local token-level confidence and entropy, read directly from the model's own output distribution at the scored tokens. This forced-suffix technique is standard in the extraction-gap literature and lets us score every prefix's observable state without needing the model to have naturally reached a conclusion at that exact point.

\subsection{Matching levels and stratified pair sampling}
\label{sec:method-matching}

Two scored prefixes, from the same problem but different trajectories, form a \textbf{matched pair at level $L$} under the following criteria.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{L1} (answer only): the two answers are identical.
  \item \textbf{L2} (plus confidence): L1, and confidence differs by at most 0.10.
  \item \textbf{L3} (plus entropy): L2, and entropy differs by at most 0.15.
\end{itemize}

All three levels additionally require the two prefixes' \textbf{normalized depth} (step index divided by total steps) to differ by at most 0.15, applied uniformly at every level. Without this, a match between step 3 of 20 and step 3 of 90 would count as different history and same state, when it may really just be a different amount of reasoning done, a depth confound rather than genuine path dependence.

From the resulting pool of matched pairs per problem and level, we sample without replacement, \textbf{stratified jointly by confidence bucket and problem identity}, a fix made after the pilot phase, where confidence stratification alone still let one problem's abundant matched pairs crowd out others sharing a confidence bucket.

\subsection{Branching and the test statistic}
\label{sec:method-branching}

For each sampled matched pair, both prefixes are continued \textbf{naturally} (no forced suffix) $N=40$ times each, up to 1500 new tokens, and parsed for a final boxed answer. A branch set must have a parse rate of at least 0.80 to enter the analysis. Below that threshold, a pair is excluded rather than silently folded into the effect-size average, per our pre-registered data-quality criterion.

For a qualifying pair, PDI is the Jensen-Shannon divergence in Equation~\ref{eq:pdi} between the two sides' 40-answer empirical distributions. The null distribution is built by pooling both sides' answers and repeatedly resplitting into groups of the original sizes (2000 permutations), recomputing PDI on each resplit. This null is constructed entirely from the pair's own data, so no noise is borrowed across pairs and no inflated sample size is introduced. The one-sided $p$-value is the fraction of resplit PDIs at least as extreme as the observed one.

\subsection{Power analysis and the branches-per-pair decision}
\label{sec:method-power}

An earlier, smaller pilot ($N=15$ branches per pair, 21 pairs) produced an uninformative null. Power simulations, confirmed independently at both a fast-approximation and full-precision level using the project's actual statistical code, showed that at 15 branches per pair, power to detect a plausible effect (a 15-point shift in future-answer probability) stayed at 3 to 9\% regardless of pair count, from 13 to 150 pairs. \textbf{Branches per pair, not pair count, is the binding lever for this design.} Raising to $N=40$ branches per pair (holding pair count at roughly 21 to 24) raises power to about 89\% at the same effect size. We fixed $N=40$ and a target of 24 pairs accordingly, an order of magnitude more branch-generation compute per pair than a naively scaled design with more, thinner pairs would spend, but the only choice that gives the pilot a real chance of detecting a modest true effect.

\subsection{Difficulty stratification and decision rule}
\label{sec:method-strat}

The 18 problems are drawn evenly (6 each) from MATH-500 difficulty levels 1 to 3, so that if path dependence correlates with problem difficulty, a priori plausible since harder problems admit longer, more divergent reasoning paths, the design can detect that rather than average it away. The 24-pair branching budget is split 8 and 8 and 8 across strata via independent per-stratum sampling calls, so no stratum can be crowded out by another.

The decision rule, fixed before any full-scale data was collected, is to reject H0 if the Fisher-combined $p$-value is below 0.05 at any matching level, reporting the specific level and stratum. Otherwise, given the design's confirmed 89\% power, we report a fail-to-reject as informative evidence against detectable path dependence at this scale, not as proof that $\phi(p)$ is sufficient in general. Effect size (mean observed PDI minus null mean) is reported regardless of significance, since, per our pre-registration, this is the number that ultimately belongs in the paper's headline, with the $p$-value gating only whether the framing is detected or not detected. \textbf{This rule is reported as pre-registered, not as what we ultimately used}: applying it produced a "not detected" verdict that a post-hoc audit (Section~\ref{sec:results-primary}) traced to a specific flaw in Fisher's method on this data, not to a genuine absence of signal. Section~\ref{sec:method-correction} describes the corrected aggregation.

\subsection{Scale-up, continuous regression, and SG-ES}
\label{sec:method-scaleup}

Three additional pieces of methodology were run after the primary design above and are reported in Sections~\ref{sec:results-scaleup} through \ref{sec:results-sges}.

First, a 45-pair scale-up using the same L1-matching pipeline, with the matching-budget bug fixed, at a reduced $N=10$ branches per pair, additionally instrumented to save each side's exact observable state and a hidden-state activation vector at the matched checkpoint.

Second, a continuous regression of PDI against state-space distance. For two matched states with confidence $c$ and entropy $e$, we define the state-space distance as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:statedist}
d(\phi_A, \phi_B) \;=\; \sqrt{(c_A - c_B)^2 + (e_A - e_B)^2},
\end{equation}
and fit an ordinary least squares regression
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:regression}
\text{PDI} \;\sim\; \beta_0 + \beta_1 \, d(\phi_A, \phi_B) + \varepsilon
\end{equation}
using this new state data, pooling all matched pairs rather than comparing discrete buckets.

Third, Sufficiency-Gated Early Stopping (SG-ES), a stopping rule that halts generation the first time an answer has been stable for 2 consecutive scored steps and confidence is at least 0.70, evaluated post-hoc against full-generation and fixed-50\%-fraction baselines computed from one densely scored natural trace per problem (19 usable MATH-500 problems, levels 1 to 4), so that all three conditions are computed from identical underlying generations rather than three separate runs.

One implementation difference from Section~\ref{sec:method-traj}'s primary-sample generation config is carried over precisely rather than smoothed away. These three additions run on a different backend code path that sets temperature 0.6 but does not explicitly pin top-$p$ to 0.95 as the primary sample's generation config does, relying instead on the checkpoint's own default (see the Reproducibility Statement for the exact configuration of each track).

\subsection{Post-hoc correction: a problem-clustered permutation test, and the regression's intercept}
\label{sec:method-correction}

Two further corrections, made after seeing the results of Section~\ref{sec:method-strat}'s pre-registered analysis, are reported in detail in Sections~\ref{sec:results-primary} and \ref{sec:results-scaleup} and described here for completeness rather than left implicit.

\textbf{The aggregation correction.} Fisher's method (Sections~\ref{sec:method-hyp}, \ref{sec:method-strat}) requires per-pair $p$-values that are continuous and informative under the null. Ours are not: any pair whose branches agree 100\% on both sides (the modal outcome, Section~\ref{sec:results-dist}) has a permutation $p$-value of exactly 1 by construction, every possible resplit of two identical multisets is itself identical, so the test has nothing left to detect at that pair, not strong evidence of a null. Pooling many such degenerate pairs with a handful of genuinely informative ones mechanically pulls Fisher's combined statistic toward 1 regardless of what the informative pairs show. We replace it with a \textbf{problem-clustered permutation test}: compute each problem's mean PDI across its own sampled pairs (so a problem contributing more pairs does not get more influence than one that contributes fewer), average across problems, and build the null for that single statistic by independently resplitting every pair's own pooled branches (same procedure as the per-pair test) and recomputing the same problem-clustered statistic on each of many replicates. This uses every pair, discards none, and never requires collapsing a pair's evidence into a binary significant-or-not call.

\textbf{The regression correction.} Section~\ref{sec:method-scaleup}'s continuous regression fits Equation~\ref{eq:regression}. H1 (path dependence exists at matched state) is a claim about divergence \emph{when} $d=0$, the intercept $\beta_0$, not about how divergence changes as $d$ grows, the slope $\beta_1$. Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup} reports both, with the intercept read as the primary quantity this paper's hypothesis actually concerns.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Primary result, as pre-registered: an apparent null that did not survive audit}
\label{sec:results-primary}

We collected 21 matched pairs across three MATH-500 difficulty strata (7 pairs per stratum), each branched 40 times per side at temperature 0.6, under L1 (answer-only) matching. Combined across all 21 pairs via Fisher's method, as pre-registered, the result is \textbf{not significant} ($\chi^2(42)$ test statistic, combined $p=0.513$). \textbf{We no longer read this as informative evidence of a null, and say so here rather than let the pre-registered number stand unchallenged.} The pre-registered decision rule (Section~\ref{sec:method-strat}) treated this as a well-powered fail-to-reject. A post-hoc audit, triggered by external review of this manuscript, found the actual explanation: 16 of these 21 pairs are exactly degenerate (PDI $=0$, Section~\ref{sec:results-dist}), which forces a permutation $p$-value of exactly 1 for each of them by construction, not because the null holds strongly there but because the test has nothing to measure. Combining 16 such mass-point-at-1 values with 5 informative ones via Fisher's method mechanically inflates the combined $p$ regardless of what the 5 actually show. Two further pieces of evidence temper the remaining two pairs specifically.

\textbf{\texttt{d1\_prob3} is a measurement artifact, not a substantive divergence.} Inspecting the raw branch outputs, one side answered "2000" on 100\% of branches, while the other answered "2000" on 78\% and \textbf{"2000calories"} on 22\%, the same numeric answer with a units word included in the boxed content. This reflects a gap in our answer normalizer rather than a real divergence.

\textbf{\texttt{d1\_prob1} and \texttt{d2\_prob1} were each independently sampled twice} at the same matching level, as two distinct matched pairs per problem, drawn from different trajectory pairs. Neither replicated on its second draw. \texttt{d1\_prob1}'s other pair scored PDI $=0.0023$, $p=0.807$. \texttt{d2\_prob1}'s other pair scored PDI $=0.066$, $p=0.058$, just short of significance. At the level of individual pairs, this non-replication is real, neither problem reliably clears the same significance threshold on every draw. We initially read this as evidence the original flags were sparse-sample noise. Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}'s corrected aggregate test complicates that reading, a genuine effect that is inconsistent pair-to-pair but nonzero on average is exactly what non-replication at the individual-pair level would also look like, so this evidence is consistent with either interpretation on its own and should not be read as having settled the question by itself.

\label{sec:results-flagged}

Both sides agree on which answer is correct. The split is in how often the model commits to stating it cleanly versus trailing off into an empty \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}, shown in Table~\ref{tab:decisive}.

Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup} reports the corrected aggregate test, which finds these two pairs, together with a third problem, are the specific, identifiable source of a small but statistically significant aggregate effect, not noise from an unlucky small sample, but not a confirmed finding either. Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} examines all three pairs directly, including these two, and reports what that examination found.

\subsection{Scale-up, the corrected aggregate test, and the continuous state-distance regression}
\label{sec:results-scaleup}

To directly address whether the two flagged pairs in Section~\ref{sec:results-flagged} reflect a real effect, we collected an independent, additional 45 matched pairs (15 per MATH-500 difficulty level, levels 1 to 3), each branched under L1 matching and instrumented to save both sides' exact observable state and a hidden-state activation vector at the matched checkpoint, instrumentation the original pilot lacked (Section~\ref{sec:lim-scope}). Budget constraints for this round fixed the per-pair branch count at $N=10$ rather than the pre-registered $N=40$, so individual pairs in this scale-up are markedly lower-power than the primary sample.

\textbf{Scale-up result, naive reading: zero nominally significant pairs.} 0 of 45 new pairs reach $p<0.05$ individually (Fisher-combined $p \approx 1.0$). 40 of 45 (89\%) show exactly PDI $=0$. Table~\ref{tab:scaleup} breaks this out by difficulty.

Difficulty level 3 already shows more nonzero variance than levels 1 and 2, consistent with the a priori hypothesis in Section~\ref{sec:method-strat}, but as with the primary sample, Fisher's method on this data is dominated by degenerate mass-at-1 $p$-values and its not-significant verdict should not be trusted at face value.

\textbf{The corrected aggregate test, on excess PDI.} Pooling the original 21 pairs with these 45 ($n=66$ total, 17 distinct problems, all L1-matched), we ran the problem-clustered permutation test described in Section~\ref{sec:method-correction}, with one further correction beyond the clustering fix itself. Jensen-Shannon divergence is non-negative, and its plug-in estimate at $N=10$ branches is biased upward for any pair that is not perfectly unanimous, so raw PDI is not directly comparable to zero. We therefore report the test on \textbf{excess PDI} (each pair's observed PDI minus the mean of that pair's own permutation null, the same quantity the regression below is built on), each problem's mean excess averaged first, then averaged across problems, tested against a null built by independently resplitting every pair's own pooled branches across many replicates.

\begin{equation}
\text{Observed statistic (mean excess PDI, problem-clustered)} = 0.0062, \quad p = 0.0005 \quad (2000 \text{ replicates})
\end{equation}

Before interpreting this as a finding, it is worth placing next to the scale it is measured on. PDI is Jensen-Shannon divergence, bounded in $[0,1]$. The observed aggregate is 0.62\% of that range. The mean within-pair permutation-null value (the typical sampling noise \emph{inside a single matched pair}, before any comparison across pairs) is 0.0098, larger than the aggregate effect itself. A statistically significant result on an effect smaller than typical single-pair sampling variance is not a contradiction (significance and effect size answer different questions at this sample size), but it is a signal to look hard at \emph{where} the effect lives before treating it as evidence of anything, which is exactly what Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} does, and where it does not survive the looking.

We report this alongside a second, more conservative test that answers a different question. The problem-clustered test above resamples only \emph{within} each pair's own branches, so it licenses the claim that these 66 pairs, taken together, diverge more than branch-sampling noise predicts, not the claim that problems in general show this effect. For the latter, we bootstrap over the 17 \emph{problems} (resampling problems with replacement, recomputing the same clustered statistic on each resample): \textbf{95\% CI on mean excess PDI: [0.0011, 0.0106]}, which excludes zero but should be read cautiously, since it is built from a sample where only 3 of 17 problems carry any nonzero value at all, so most resamples' variance comes from how often those 3 problems happen to be drawn, not from independent evidence across a broader set of problems. We report both tests because they answer different questions and neither should be asked to answer the other.

The result is robust to how pairs are weighted (a naive pair-weighted statistic, no problem clustering, gives the same order of significance), to excluding \texttt{d3\_prob5} (Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}), and to normalizing all raw answers through our answer normalizer first (removing the known "2000" vs "2000calories" artifact, and a similar single-character truncation artifact affecting one \texttt{d2\_prob1} pair, "evelyn"/"7/5" vs "evelyn"/"e", the "e" almost certainly a truncated "evelyn", that Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} also flags as part of that problem's disposal). We also split the pooled 66 pairs by generation configuration (Section~\ref{sec:method-scaleup} notes the two tracks differ in whether top-$p$ is pinned to 0.95): the 21-pair primary sample (top-$p=0.95$) gives $p=0.0005$ on its own, while the 45-pair scale-up (default top-$p$) gives $p=0.14$, not significant on its own. Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} checks each of the three problems carrying this signal directly, and none of the three holds up.

\textbf{Where the signal lives.} Breaking the 17 problems down individually: \textbf{14 show exactly PDI $=0$ in every one of their sampled pairs.} All of the aggregate signal comes from 3 problems, \texttt{d1\_prob1} (4 pairs, mean PDI 0.016), \texttt{d2\_prob1} (6 pairs, mean PDI 0.090), and \texttt{d3\_prob5} (4 pairs, mean PDI 0.132, every single pair nonzero). Figure~\ref{fig:perproblem} shows all 17 problems side by side. Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} checks each of these three directly and finds a specific, disposable explanation for all three.

On reinspection this was not a defensible reading, and we correct it here rather than let it stand. Of the 45 regression pairs, \textbf{7 sit at exactly zero state-distance} (both sides matching on confidence and entropy too, not by design but because both had already fully committed to an answer), which is a \emph{direct measurement} at the exact point the intercept is meant to estimate, not an extrapolation. \textbf{All 7 show zero excess PDI.} Refitting excess-PDI on Equation~\ref{eq:regression} with cluster-robust standard errors on the full 45 pairs gives

\begin{equation}
\text{intercept } \beta_0 = 0.0022 \; (\text{SE } 0.0025), \; p=0.38 \quad \text{slope } \beta_1 = -0.0011 \; (\text{SE } 0.0014), \; p=0.44 \quad (R^2 = 0.005, \; n=45)

both null, and the intercept is now close to the direct zero-distance measurement rather than pulled upward from it. The earlier 0.0214 figure came from fitting the \emph{raw}, upward-biased PDI rather than excess PDI, and from a regression line fit across the full 0 to 2.3 range of observed distances despite the data being sparse near zero everywhere except those 7 exact points, an extrapolation problem compounded by an estimation-bias problem. Figure~\ref{fig:contreg} marks these 7 direct measurements distinctly from the fitted line. \textbf{Mechanistic probe: uninformative, not negative, and a live alternative explanation, not just a footnote.} We also attempted the hidden-state decisiveness probe motivated by Section~\ref{sec:results-flagged}'s original hypothesis (is "will this prefix state a decisive answer" linearly decodable from its hidden state?). It could not be trained: across all 90 labeled examples (45 pairs times 2 sides), every single branch set produced a non-empty answer, zero "trails off empty" examples at $N=10$ branches per pair, so the label has no variance for a classifier to learn from. We do not read this as evidence the decisiveness gap is rare in general. Section~\ref{sec:method-scaleup} already discloses that the scale-up ran on a different backend code path that does not pin top-$p$ to 0.95, unlike the primary sample. If that configuration difference shifts the generation distribution even modestly, it could independently explain why empty-box outputs, common enough in the primary sample to produce \texttt{d1\_prob1} and \texttt{d2\_prob1}'s decisiveness gaps, did not recur even once across 90 branch sets here, a rate that would be statistically implausible (well under 1\% chance) if the primary sample's apparent 10 to 25\% empty rates were representative of the true underlying rate under this scale-up's actual generation settings. We cannot distinguish "the effect is genuinely rare" from "the backend configuration change suppressed it" with the data we have, and flag both as live explanations rather than picking one.

\subsection{Three apparent exceptions, and why none survives}
\label{sec:results-mechanisms}

Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}'s aggregate test is statistically significant and substantively tiny: 0.0062 mean excess PDI against a 0.0098 within-pair noise floor, meaning the aggregate effect is smaller than the typical sampling variation inside a single pair, on a metric bounded at 1. All of it comes from 3 of 17 problems. A significant $p$-value on a small, concentrated effect is exactly the situation where the effect deserves direct scrutiny before it is reported as a finding, not after. This section is that scrutiny, applied to each of the three problems in turn. \textbf{None of the three survives it.} We had, at earlier points in this project, proposed two mechanisms to explain this divergence and reported one of them as the paper's central result. Both mechanisms were tested directly. Both failed. What is left is a diagnosis of why the aggregate test found something it should not be read as having found, not a positive account of anything.

\textbf{\texttt{d1\_prob3}: a normalizer artifact, already known.} One side answered "2000" on every branch; the other answered "2000" on 78\% of branches and "2000calories" (the same number with a trailing unit) on the remainder. Our answer normalizer does not strip trailing non-numeric tokens. Running the aggregate test on normalized rather than raw strings removes this pair's contribution to the signal entirely (Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}). This one is disposed of and was flagged as such from the first pass; we restate it here only so the three problems are handled in one place.

\textbf{\texttt{d1\_prob1}, \texttt{d2\_prob1}: the decisiveness gap, tied to one generation configuration, not to history.} Both problems show one side of a matched pair stating a clean answer while the other trails off into an empty \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}. This signal is confined entirely to the 21-pair primary sample (top-$p=0.95$, $p=0.0005$ on that track alone) and is absent from the 45-pair scale-up (default top-$p$, $p=0.14$). We designed a targeted audit to test whether this is token-budget right-censoring (branches capped at 1500 tokens; both problems are long-output problems where a less-advanced prefix plausibly needs more tokens to close its box). The first version of that audit, through an implementation oversight, ran under the wrong generation configuration (the scale-up's default top-$p$, not the primary sample's 0.95) and so could not settle the question it was built for, though it did rule out censoring under the configuration it happened to test: across 160 fresh branches, every one terminated by genuine end-of-sequence, none hit the doubled 3000-token cap, and every branch converged regardless of confidence gaps as wide as 0.811 versus 0.102. A corrected rerun, with top-$p$ pinned to 0.95 to match the primary sample's actual configuration, is reported in Section~\ref{sec:lim-caution} with the result folded in here: [AUDIT\_RESULT\_PLACEHOLDER]. What is already clear regardless: this signal tracks a sampling-configuration switch, not a difference in history at matched observable state. A phenomenon that appears under one decoding setting and vanishes under another, with nothing about the matching changing between the two, is evidence about decoding configuration, not evidence against state sufficiency.

\textbf{\texttt{d3\_prob5}: the effect is inside the noise of a single prefix resampled against itself.} This is the strongest apparent finding in the dataset, and the one we scrutinized hardest, because it is the one that survived longest. In the original 4 sampled pairs, 3 showed the lower-confidence side of a pair scattering into wrong answers while the higher-confidence side stayed correct, a 10 to 30 percentage-point accuracy gap, shown in Table~\ref{tab:errorscatter}.

\emph{First check: does confidence actually explain it?} We ran a properly-powered follow-up on 10 fresh \texttt{d3\_prob5} trajectories, drawing 4 new L1 (answer-only) pairs and 4 new L2 (answer-and-confidence-matched) pairs from the same shared generation run. If confidence were the explanation, L2 pairs should show little to no divergence, since confidence is already matched within 0.10. They do not: one L2 pair (confidence 0.426 versus 0.493, a difference of 0.067, well inside tolerance) still shows real divergence (PDI $=0.169$), direct evidence that matching confidence does not close the gap. And the one divergent L1 pair in the follow-up went in the \emph{opposite} direction from the original pattern, the higher-confidence side scattered, not the lower-confidence side. That is a real methodological result in its own right, not merely the absence of one: an aggregate test can return a small but significant $p$-value, on data pooled across pairs that reuse the same handful of underlying trajectories, and the specific story built to explain where that signal lives can fail to survive direct scrutiny at every problem it touches. \subsection{A stopping rule built on confidence does not beat the simplest baseline, the practical payoff}
\label{sec:results-sges}

If observable state is sufficient for early stopping, as Sections~\ref{sec:results-primary} through~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} argue, the practical implication is direct: a stopping rule should not need anything more sophisticated than simple answer extraction, and adding a confidence gate on top of it should buy nothing. We test this directly with \textbf{Sufficiency-Gated Early Stopping (SG-ES)}: stop generation the first time an answer has been stable for 2 consecutive scored steps \emph{and} confidence at that step exceeds 0.70, a rule motivated directly by the kind of L1/L2 comparison this paper's design is built around. We compare it against two baselines computed post-hoc from the same dense, step-scored trace, \textbf{full generation} (run to completion) and \textbf{forced extraction at a fixed 50\% depth} (the naive rule, ignoring confidence entirely), on 19 usable MATH-500 problems (levels 1 to 4, 5 of 24 sampled problems dropped for 0 scored prefixes, the same forced-extraction failure mode noted throughout this paper). \section{Discussion: Why Might This Sufficiency Hold?}
\label{sec:discussion}

Everything in Section~\ref{sec:results} is a black-box behavioral result. We observe inputs and outputs, never gradients, attention, or routing internals (our one attempt to look inside, the hidden-state decisiveness probe in Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}, could not train on this round's data). What follows is therefore explicitly a \textbf{hypothesis for future mechanistic work, not a claim this paper tests}. We flag it as such throughout rather than letting the confident tone of the rest of the paper leak into speculation we have not verified.

\subsection{A training-time funneling hypothesis}
\label{sec:discussion-funnel}

DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, the model used throughout this paper, is not itself RL-trained. As reported in the DeepSeek-R1 technical report \citep{deepseekr1_2025}, the distilled model family is produced by supervised fine-tuning of a Qwen2.5 base checkpoint on reasoning trajectories \emph{generated by} the RL-trained (GRPO) DeepSeek-R1 teacher. The 7B student never sees an RL objective directly. It imitates the teacher's already-produced outputs. This two-stage lineage suggests a candidate explanation for why sufficiency holds where it does, at least for this specific model.

GRPO optimizes the teacher's policy using a reward that is, in DeepSeek-R1's reported setup, a function of the \emph{final} answer's correctness (plus formatting), evaluated across a group of sampled rollouts from the same prompt and normalized within-group. Critically, this reward signal has no mechanism to distinguish between two rollouts that reach the same correct answer by different reasoning paths. RL gradient pressure under this objective can shape \emph{when and how confidently} a trajectory commits to an answer, but has no way to independently reward or penalize \emph{which path} it took to get there, once the destination is fixed. If this group-relative, outcome-only objective causes the teacher's policy to converge many different reasoning paths onto a narrow, low-variance continuation distribution once enough evidence has accumulated for a given answer, a plausible mechanism, though not one we test directly, then the teacher's own training data, used to fine-tune the distilled student, would already exhibit exactly the behavioral pattern this paper measures: path-independence of future output conditional on current $\phi(p)$. The student, trained only to imitate that already-funneled distribution, would inherit the pattern without needing any RL stage of its own.

This framing makes a falsifiable prediction for future work, which we regard as the most direct way to test the hypothesis rather than just argue for it. \textbf{Path dependence, if it exists, should be more likely to appear in models whose training does not include this kind of outcome-only, group-relative shaping}, for example a base model continuing raw chain-of-thought with no reasoning-specific fine-tuning, or a policy trained with an explicit diversity or entropy bonus over intermediate reasoning rather than an outcome-only reward. Our finding would then be best read not as a property of "reasoning models" as a category, but as a downstream consequence of a specific, now-common training recipe, a sharper and more useful claim than the more general one, and consistent with the generalization caution in Section~\ref{sec:lim-general}.

This hypothesis is offered to explain the paper's actual finding, near-total convergence across 66 pairs, not the three apparent exceptions Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} examines and disposes of. We had, at an earlier point in this project, read those three exceptions as the place funneling should be expected to fail, and built a correspondingly specific prediction around them. Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} shows none of the three is a genuine exception, which removes the evidence that prediction was built on. The funneling hypothesis survives as an explanation for the finding this paper actually supports; what it does not have, after Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}, is a documented failure case within this dataset to sharpen it against. The concrete next step remains replicating this design on a directly RL-trained, non-distilled model, now framed as a direct test of whether funneling explains sufficiency in general rather than as a search for where it locally breaks down.

\subsection{Reconciling with RAD: representational divergence without behavioral divergence}
\label{sec:discussion-rad}

RAD \citep{rad2026} (Section~\ref{sec:relatedwork}) reports that identical output tokens do not imply identical internal state. Hidden activations and mixture-of-experts routing decisions can differ even when the surface token stream matches. Taken at face value, this could look like it contradicts our finding. If internal state still diverges after two trajectories agree on a token, how can we also find that their \emph{future} behavior converges regardless of history?

We think these findings are compatible, not in tension, once "state" is disambiguated. Our $\phi(p)$ is a deliberately low-dimensional, behaviorally defined summary (answer, confidence, entropy), a projection of whatever the model's full internal state is, chosen because it is exactly what existing early-stopping methods already compute and act on. RAD's internal state is much higher-dimensional and representationally complete. It is entirely possible, and if RAD's finding holds, likely, for the full internal state to remain genuinely path-dependent, meaning the model "remembers," in its activations or routing, something about how it got here, while that residual memory is behaviorally inert for the specific downstream question this paper asks: what final answer will this trajectory eventually state. A sufficient statistic is only sufficient \emph{for a specific target}. $\phi(p)$ can be sufficient for predicting the eventual answer while being insufficient for predicting other properties (response style, verbosity, robustness to a rephrased question, or exactly the routing-level facts RAD's probing methodology is built to detect) that a hidden-state-level analysis would still find path-dependent.

This is also, concretely, what our own attempted hidden-state decisiveness probe (Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}) was designed to test, whether hidden state carries decisiveness-relevant information \emph{beyond} $\phi(p)$, and it remains the most direct way to adjudicate this question on our own data and pipeline, rather than by analogy to a different paper's setup. It could not train this round because the label had no variance at $N=10$ branches. Rerunning it at the pre-registered $N=40$, specifically targeting problems and matching levels known to produce some non-decisive branches (\texttt{d2\_prob1} and \texttt{d1\_prob1} from Section~\ref{sec:results-dist}), is the concrete next experiment we would run to test this section's hypothesis directly rather than only discuss it.

\subsection{A retired hypothesis, noted for the record}
\label{sec:discussion-subject}

An earlier draft of this section proposed that MATH-500's "Intermediate Algebra" label predicted path dependence, on the strength of both Intermediate Algebra problems in our 17-problem set being among the three apparent exceptions. Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} shows those three exceptions do not hold up on direct inspection, which removes the pattern this hypothesis was built to explain. We are not carrying it forward. We note it here, rather than deleting it silently, because it is a clean example of the general risk this paper's own revisions illustrate: a correlate built from $n=2$ is cheap to find and easy to find a story for, and the story survives only as long as nobody checks the underlying cases directly.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}

\subsection{Scope: discrete matching levels remain L1-only; the continuous test is now run}
\label{sec:lim-scope}

Our design pre-registered testing discrete L1, L2, and L3 matching and reporting whether richer state resolves any gap found at the loosest level. Due to an implementation bug, Phase 3's pair-branching loop shared one sampling budget across all three matching levels instead of giving each an independent budget, so L1 always exhausted it before L2 or L3 were ever sampled. \textbf{All 66 pairs in this paper (21 primary, 45 scale-up) are L1 (answer-only) matched.} The bug itself is fixed and verified (a follow-up L2/L3 collection in Section~\ref{sec:results-discrete-check} used the corrected code path successfully), but we did not spend further budget on a full L2/L3 stratified sweep at scale, because Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}'s continuous state-distance regression answers the underlying question (does richer state resolve divergence?) directly and more informatively than discrete L2/L3 buckets would, without needing separate matching-level datasets. \textbf{The one thing that remains genuinely untested is whether a full-scale discrete L2/L3 design would qualitatively agree with the continuous regression}; we have no reason to expect it would disagree, but we have not run it and do not claim otherwise.

\subsection{Achieved sample size: 21 of 24 pre-registered pairs}
\label{sec:lim-samplesize}

One difficulty-3 chunk's two problems initially produced 0 usable matched pairs (0 of 500 forced-extractions parsed, because the natural reasoning trace was truncating before reaching a conclusion at the original 2000-token budget), triggering our own pre-registered inconclusive-data flag ($>25\%$ of attempted pairs dropped by the parse-rate gate). We diagnosed and fixed this (raising the trace and scoring token budgets) and reran, but the rerun yielded only 1 pair for that chunk rather than the targeted 3, leaving the final dataset 3 pairs short of the 24-pair target. We report the achieved sample of 21 pairs (7 per stratum) rather than treating 24 as met.

\subsection{The flagged pairs: noise, then signal, then examined and disposed of --- three readings, and what each stage should teach a reader}
\label{sec:lim-caution}

This project reported three different readings of the same 3 anomalous pairs, in sequence, and we document all three rather than let the current one stand as if it were the first. \textbf{Reading 1} (an earlier draft): \texttt{d1\_prob1} and \texttt{d2\_prob1}'s flags were resolved as sparse-sample noise, on the strength of Bonferroni correction, non-replication, and a slope-only regression reading. Each of those three arguments was itself flawed---Fisher's method mechanically favors ``not significant'' on data this degenerate (Section~\ref{sec:results-primary}); non-replication is equally consistent with a real-but-inconsistent effect; the regression argument conflated slope with intercept (Section~\ref{sec:method-correction})---and a corrected, problem-clustered test found these same two pairs, plus a third (\texttt{d3\_prob5}), were the source of a statistically significant aggregate effect. \textbf{Reading 2}: this was reported as a real, if minority, finding, and became the paper's central claim for one full revision. \textbf{Reading 3}, reached by directly testing the two mechanisms Reading 2 proposed rather than reporting them at face value: both tests came back against the mechanisms, and Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} shows each of the three pairs has a specific, disposable explanation instead---a normalizer artifact, a sampling-configuration effect, and a resampling-noise artifact of one prefix counted as multiple independent observations.

We are reporting all three readings, not just the last one, because the lesson generalizes beyond this dataset: \textbf{a discrete significant/not-significant framing over many pairs, most of which are structurally degenerate, is a genuinely easy way to talk yourself into a null that isn't there---and a small, statistically significant aggregate effect concentrated in a handful of pairs is an equally easy way to talk yourself into a finding that isn't there, unless each pair carrying the signal is checked directly rather than accepted because the aggregate test passed.} Any future work using this kind of matched-pair design should default to a continuous, problem-clustered aggregate statistic (Section~\ref{sec:method-correction}) rather than combining discrete per-pair $p$-values, should treat a discrete method's ``no effect'' verdict as provisional until checked against one, and should treat a small aggregate effect's ``effect'' verdict as equally provisional until every pair carrying it is inspected individually, the way Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} inspects these three.

\textbf{A targeted audit for the reversed finding's weaker mechanism, and a mistake in how we ran it.} The reversal above resolved \texttt{d1\_prob1} and \texttt{d2\_prob1} from ``noise'' to ``signal,'' but Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}'s track split shows that resolution itself needs one more check: the decisiveness-gap signal in these two problems is confined to the top-$p=0.95$ track and could plausibly be token-budget right-censoring rather than a genuine history effect (Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}). We designed a direct test: regenerate branches for both problems at double the original 1500-token budget, recording the exact token index at which each \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} closes (or whether it closes at all), which lets us compute what the empty-answer rate would have been at any earlier cutoff from a single round of generation. We committed in advance to report whichever answer it returned rather than let the reversed finding stand unaudited simply because it is now the more interesting story, and that commitment caught a real mistake in the audit itself, not just in the original finding. The audit script reused this project's default backend configuration, which does not pin top-$p$, so it ran under the scale-up track's configuration rather than the primary sample's top-$p=0.95$. The result it returned is genuinely informative, censoring is ruled out under the configuration tested (all 160 fresh branches terminated by natural end-of-sequence, none hit the 3000-token cap, and every branch converged regardless of confidence gaps as wide as 0.811 versus 0.102), but it does not test what we set out to test, since it was not run at top-$p=0.95$. We report this exactly as it happened: the audit succeeded at ruling out one hypothesis (censoring, under this configuration) while failing, through our own implementation error, to directly test the one we most needed to (censoring, at the primary sample's actual configuration). A corrected rerun, generating branches at top-$p=0.95$ and the doubled token budget together, was launched to settle this directly. It completed all 4 planned pairs (the first attempt at \texttt{d2\_prob1} was interrupted by a client-side connection drop and was rerun separately). Across the resulting 320 fresh branches, exactly one was empty at 1500 tokens, a single side of one \texttt{d2\_prob1} pair, and it closed by 3000 tokens: a direct, confirmed instance of token-budget censoring. Every other branch, 319 of 320, was already non-empty at 1500. So censoring is real as a mechanism, we caught it happening once, but it accounts for a small fraction of what the original sample showed: an independent resample of similar late-stage prefixes reproduced roughly 0.3\% empties here against 5.8\% in the original \texttt{d1\_prob1}/\texttt{d2\_prob1} branches. Most of the original gap did not reproduce. We read this as a third case that does not survive scrutiny at anything like its original size, in the same spirit as \texttt{d3\_prob5} above.

\subsection{The continuous regression: corrected, and what's still open}
\label{sec:lim-continuous}

Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup} reports the continuous state-distance regression this section previously flagged as identified-but-not-run, and withdraws our own earlier reading of its intercept. An earlier draft reported the intercept at 0.0214, $p=0.086$, and read it as ``closer to significance'' than the null slope suggested. On reinspection that number came from fitting raw, upward-biased PDI rather than excess PDI, and from extrapolating a regression line across the full range of observed state-distances despite the data being sparse everywhere near zero except at 7 pairs measured \emph{directly} at zero distance, all 7 of which show zero divergence. The corrected regression, on excess PDI with cluster-robust standard errors, gives an intercept of 0.0022 ($p=0.38$), consistent with the direct zero-distance measurement rather than pulled above it, and a null slope ($p=0.44$). We now treat the 7 pairs measured directly at zero distance, not the fitted line, as the evidence that speaks to H1. What remains open: this regression pools only the 45 scale-up pairs (the 21 primary pairs lack saved state and cannot be added retroactively), it was run at a reduced $N=10$ branches/pair rather than the pre-registered 40, and---like everything else in this paper---it reflects one model on one domain. A higher-power replication of the continuous regression specifically, with state persisted from the start and the full $N=40$ branch budget, is the most direct way to raise confidence in the intercept estimate beyond what we report here.

\subsection{Generalization}
\label{sec:lim-general}

All results---the primary sample, the scale-up, the corrected aggregate test, the continuous regression, and the SG-ES comparison---are from a single model (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B) on a single dataset (MATH-500). We report sufficiency of observable state within this scope; we do not claim it as a general property of observable state across reasoning model families, sizes, or task domains (e.g., GSM8K, commonsense reasoning), and readers should not extend our framing that far on their own. This paper licenses ``for this model and this domain, current-state early-stopping is safe, including for the specific cases that initially looked like exceptions''---not a claim about whether the same holds for other models, domains, or training recipes, which Section~\ref{sec:discussion-funnel}'s funneling hypothesis gives a specific, falsifiable reason to doubt without testing directly.

\subsection{Two stress tests we ran against our own null, and what they found}
\label{sec:lim-stresstests}

Having withdrawn the positive finding in Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}, we checked two further ways it might have been concealing something rather than genuinely absent---both prompted directly by points raised in external review.

\textbf{Could the answer normalizer be manufacturing zeros?} Sections~\ref{sec:results-dist} and~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} already document one direction of normalizer error (a genuine divergence collapsed by trailing-unit or truncation artifacts). We checked the opposite direction across the full dataset: for every one of the 66 pairs, computing PDI on raw, un-normalized strings and comparing it to PDI on normalized strings. If normalization were systematically collapsing genuinely distinct answers onto one string, this would show up as zero-PDI pairs on normalized text that are nonzero on raw text. It did not: 56 of 66 pairs are already zero on raw strings, before any normalization runs, and only one pair in the entire dataset (the known \texttt{d1\_prob3} artifact) has its PDI changed by normalization at all, and in the expected direction. The null majority is not a normalizer artifact.

\textbf{Is right-censoring suppressing divergence globally, not just in the two problems Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} examines?} Branches are capped at 1500 tokens against 2000--3000 token traces, so every branch set is censored relative to its own generation in principle. We checked the empty-answer rate (the observable footprint of censoring) across all 66 pairs, not just the two where we designed a targeted audit. It is not diffuse: 150 of 2,580 branches are empty overall (5.8\%), and every one of them comes from \texttt{d1\_prob1} or \texttt{d2\_prob1}. The other 15 problems have \textbf{zero empty answers across 2,140 branches}. If the 1500-token cap were suppressing divergence broadly, we would expect at least some empties scattered across the null-majority problems too. We do not observe that.

Both checks came back in favor of the null being genuine rather than an artifact of measurement. We report them for the same reason we report everything else that could have gone against us: a null we tried twice to break, using specific mechanisms an external review proposed, and could not, is more trustworthy than a null we simply failed to interrogate.

\subsection{A real, unresolved measurement limitation: matching on a noisy proxy}
\label{sec:lim-noisystate}

One limitation neither of the checks above addresses, and which we cannot fully rule out with the current design: $\phi(p)$ is read off a single 64-token forced-extraction continuation (Section~\ref{sec:method-traj}), which is itself a sample with real variance in confidence and entropy, not the trajectory's true underlying state. Matching two prefixes as ``equal'' on a noisy estimate means some fraction of nominally matched pairs are not actually matched, and any residual divergence this produces would look identical, in our data, to genuine history-dependence. Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}'s within-prefix resampling check (three branch sets from one literal, unambiguous prefix) rules out noisy matching as the explanation for that specific case, since there was no matching involved at all, one prefix was resampled against itself. It does not rule out noisy-$\phi$ as a contributor to the small number of other nonzero pairs elsewhere in the dataset. A design that estimates $\phi$ from multiple forced extractions per checkpoint, averaging out this specific source of noise, would close this gap directly and is the most concrete methodological improvement we would make if we ran this design again.

\subsection{Pipeline reliability}
\label{sec:lim-pipeline}

Over the course of this pilot we found and fixed several implementation bugs that materially affected results before being caught: a library version incompatibility that silently crashed generation, an auto-selected numerical precision that degraded output quality by 65--82\% before being pinned explicitly, and the matching-level budget bug in Section~\ref{sec:lim-scope}. We do not consider the current pipeline guaranteed free of further such issues, and note this as a general caution for interpreting any single pilot's numbers as final---particularly ones, like this one, run under a fixed compute budget across heterogeneous infrastructure.

\subsection{Anticipated objections}
\label{sec:lim-objections}

We list the five critiques of this design we consider most serious, in our own words as if made by a skeptical reviewer, and respond to each directly rather than only in aggregate.

\textbf{Objection 1.} The 45-pair scale-up was run at $N=10$ branches per pair, a quarter of the pre-registered $N=40$ that Section~\ref{sec:method-power}'s own power analysis said was necessary. At $N=15$, that analysis measured only 3 to 9\% power to detect the targeted effect size, regardless of pair count. Individual pairs in the scale-up are not independently powered to detect the pre-registered effect size, and a reader should not treat any single scale-up pair's non-significance, or significance, at face value.

This is correct as stated about \emph{per-pair} power, and we do not dispute the math, we say so explicitly in Sections~\ref{sec:method-scaleup} and~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}. But the paper's central result no longer rests on any individual scale-up pair clearing a significance threshold, it rests on Section~\ref{sec:method-correction}'s problem-clustered aggregate test, whose power comes from pooling continuous divergence values across 66 pairs and 17 problems, not from any one pair's branch count. We do not claim the scale-up alone settles anything about the confidence and entropy axis with high confidence, Section~\ref{sec:lim-continuous} says a higher-power replication at $N=40$ is the single most valuable follow-up to this paper, not a defense of the current numbers as final.

\textbf{Objection 2.} The paper is framed around observable state sufficiency in general, but the only well-powered test, the primary 21-pair sample, uses L1 (answer-only) matching exclusively, due to the budget bug in Section~\ref{sec:lim-scope}. A reader could reasonably think confidence and entropy were tested with the same rigor as the answer, when in fact the only test that touches them directly is the lower-power, $N=10$ continuous regression.

This remains a fair and precise criticism even after this paper's other corrections. We have tried to keep the paper's language tracking this rather than blurring it: Sections~\ref{sec:results-primary} through~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} state the null finding holds for L1 matching, and separately flag that whether confidence and entropy matching would change the picture is not yet well-powered evidence, only the directional intercept result. The concrete fix, not yet run, is a full-scale discrete L2/L3 sweep or a continuous regression at the pre-registered $N=40$, both already listed as the paper's top future-work item.

\textbf{Objection 3.} DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B is a distilled model, trained via supervised fine-tuning on a teacher's already-generated reasoning traces. Output homogeneity, meaning low diversity and a narrow trajectory distribution, is a known property of distillation generally. A near-zero path-dependence result on a model whose training objective may already collapse trajectory diversity could simply be measuring the distillation process, not a general property of reasoning models, and would not be expected to replicate on a directly RL-trained, non-distilled model with a more diverse rollout distribution.

This is, in our own assessment, the single largest external-validity threat to this paper, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion-funnel} engages it head-on rather than treating it as a footnote: we propose training lineage, meaning outcome-only, group-relative RL shaping the teacher, then supervised fine-tuning distilling that into the student, as a candidate mechanism for why sufficiency holds where it does. We did not test a non-distilled, directly RL-trained model in this paper, and we do not claim our result generalizes to one.

\textbf{Objection 4.} This paper's own narrative shows the corrected, significant aggregate result was found by building a new statistical test \emph{after} external review flagged a problem with the original one, on the same data, without pre-registering the replacement. Then, when a follow-up test killed the mechanisms that explained it, the paper pivoted \emph{again} to a null claim. That is close to the textbook definition of a questionable research practice, construct analyses until one tells a clean story, then report that one as the finding.

We agree with the premise, changing tests and reversing claims post-hoc is dangerous, which is why Appendix~\ref{sec:appendixa} documents every deviation from the pre-registration and the sequence of claim reversals explicitly, rather than presenting the final analysis as if it were the first one we tried. The pivot back to the null was driven by direct, pre-committed tests of the proposed mechanisms failing (Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}), not by a desire for a cleaner story. We believe the paper's current state, a null that survived two direct stress-tests (Section~\ref{sec:lim-stresstests}), is more robust than a positive finding built on a single, unaudited reading of a post-hoc test.

\textbf{Objection 5.} A model's observable output is not its internal state. The model's forward pass depends on the full KV cache of the context, which differs structurally between two histories even if their last sentence matches. Claiming ``observable state is a sufficient statistic'' is category error.

We agree, and clarify this in Section~\ref{sec:method-hyp}: we are not testing sufficiency in the strict Markovian sense over the internal representations (which are trivially distinct), we are testing it as a behavioral claim over the distribution of final answers. The paper's question is whether the observable text provides enough information to make an early-stopping decision, not whether the model has "forgotten" the history in its internal activations. Section~\ref{sec:discussion-rad} specifically addresses how these two can diverge.

\section*{Reproducibility Statement}

All experiments use a single open-source model, \textbf{DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B} (publicly available checkpoint, no fine-tuning applied), and a single public benchmark, \textbf{MATH-500}. Trajectory generation uses temperature 0.6 throughout, at fp16 precision (forced explicitly, not auto-selected---an earlier auto-selected bf16 run on different hardware degraded output quality by 65--82\% before this was caught and pinned; see Section~\ref{sec:lim-pipeline}). Top-$p$ is explicitly set to 0.95 in the generation config used for the pre-registered 21-pair primary sample; the 45-pair scale-up and the SG-ES evaluation (Sections~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}, \ref{sec:results-sges}) use the same temperature but do not explicitly override top-$p$, relying instead on the checkpoint's own default generation config---we did not verify this resolves to the same value and flag it here as a genuine configuration difference between the two collection tracks rather than assume equivalence. A fully precise replication of the primary sample should pin \texttt{top\_p=0.95} explicitly, as the released generation scripts for that track do. 

Token budgets: up to 3000 new tokens for full reasoning trajectories (2000 for the majority collected before a one-difficulty-stratum-specific increase, documented in Section~\ref{sec:lim-samplesize}), up to 64 new tokens for forced-extraction $\phi(p)$ scoring, and up to 1500 new tokens per natural branch continuation. Matching tolerances: confidence $\le 0.10$ (L2), entropy $\le 0.15$ (L3), normalized depth $\le 0.15$ (all levels). Branch counts: $N=40$ per side for the pre-registered 21-pair primary sample and the exploratory L2/L3 follow-up (Section~\ref{sec:results-discrete-check}); $N=10$ per side for the 45-pair scale-up (Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}) and the SG-ES evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:results-sges}), a reduced budget whose effect on statistical power is discussed explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:lim-objections}. The permutation null for the Path Dependence Index uses 2000 resplits per pair, and the problem-clustered aggregate test on excess PDI (Section~\ref{sec:method-correction}) uses 2000 replicates. Difficulty stratification uses MATH-500's own provided \texttt{level} field (1--4).

All trajectory-generation and branching scripts, the forced-extraction $\phi(p)$ scoring code, the continuous state-distance regression code, the problem-clustered permutation test (Sections~\ref{sec:method-correction}, \ref{sec:results-scaleup}) with its robustness-check variants (naive pair-weighting, problem exclusion, answer normalization, track split), the SG-ES stopping-rule implementation and its unit tests, the hidden-state-decisiveness logistic-regression probe, and the raw parsed branch-outcome logs underlying every number and figure in this paper are released at \url{https://github.com/Sharkyii/cot-path-dependence}. \texttt{scripts/final\_analysis.py} reproduces every statistic in Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup} (excess PDI, the problem-clustered test, the cluster-robust regression, and the track split) from the raw result files in one run. The Section~\ref{sec:lim-caution} audit's scripts (\texttt{modal\_audit1a\_trajectories.py}, \texttt{modal\_audit1b\_branch.py} for the confidence-matching contrast on \texttt{d3\_prob5}, and \texttt{modal\_audit2a\_trajectories.py}, \texttt{modal\_audit2b\_branch.py} for the censoring check on \texttt{d1\_prob1} and \texttt{d2\_prob1}) are released alongside a zero-cost, zero-GPU smoke test (\texttt{scripts/audit\_smoke\_test.py}) that exercises the same orchestration code against a mock backend.

\section{Deviations and Corrections}
\label{sec:appendixa}

This paper's conclusions did not arrive in one pass. We report the final analysis in the body and collect every substantive deviation from the pre-registered plan, and every correction to our own earlier reading of the data, here---dated, in order, with the reasoning for each---rather than thread the revision history through the results sections themselves.

\textbf{2026-08 (pre-registration).} Design specifies discrete L1/L2/L3 matching, $N=40$ branches per pair, 24 pairs (8 per stratum), Fisher's method for combining per-pair $p$-values, decision rule: reject H0 if combined $p < 0.05$ at any level.

\textbf{Matching-budget bug (found during primary collection).} The pair-branching loop shared one sampling budget across all three matching levels instead of giving each an independent budget, so L1 always exhausted it first. All 66 pairs in this paper are L1 (answer-only) matched. Fixed and verified in the code (Section~\ref{sec:lim-scope}), but never spent further budget on a full L2/L3 sweep at scale, since the continuous regression (Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}) answers the underlying confidence/entropy question directly. \textbf{Still open}, and now this paper's top remaining future-work item (Objection 2, Section~\ref{sec:lim-objections}).

\textbf{Sample-size shortfall.} One difficulty-3 chunk initially produced 0 usable matched pairs from a token-budget-truncation issue; the fix and rerun yielded 21 pairs against a 24-pair target (Section~\ref{sec:lim-samplesize}). Reported as achieved $N$, not backfilled.

\textbf{Primary-sample result, as pre-registered: Fisher's combined $p = 0.513$, read at the time as a well-powered null (Section~\ref{sec:results-primary}).}

\textbf{Reading 1 (an earlier draft): reversed to noise-attributed dismissal.} Three pairs reached nominal per-pair significance; this was argued away via Bonferroni correction, non-replication on a second sampled pair per problem, and a slope-only regression reading. All three arguments were later shown to be flawed (Sections~\ref{sec:results-primary}, \ref{sec:method-correction}, \ref{sec:lim-caution}).

\textbf{Discovery: Fisher's method degenerates on this data.} External review identified that 56 of 66 permutation $p$-values are pinned at exactly 1 by mathematical construction (both sides of a pair agreeing 100\% of the time), which mechanically drags any Fisher-style combined statistic toward ``no effect'' regardless of what the informative pairs show (Sections~\ref{sec:method-hyp}, \ref{sec:results-primary}). Verified by direct computation before accepting it.

\textbf{Correction 1: problem-clustered permutation test replaces Fisher's method.} Built on continuous divergence values, avoiding the degenerate-$p$-value failure mode entirely (Section~\ref{sec:method-correction}). Initial result on raw PDI: $T = 0.0149$, $p = 0.00005$.

\textbf{Correction 2: excess-PDI bias fix.} Jensen-Shannon divergence's plug-in estimator is biased upward at $N=10$ for non-unanimous pairs, so raw PDI is not comparable to zero. Every subsequent test in this paper operates on excess PDI (observed minus that pair's own permutation null mean) instead. Corrected result: mean excess PDI = 0.0062, $p = 0.0005$ (Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}).

\textbf{Reading 2: reported as a real, minority, structurally identifiable finding.} Two candidate mechanisms proposed: a decisiveness gap (\texttt{d1\_prob1}, \texttt{d2\_prob1}) and an error-scatter, confidence-linked pattern (\texttt{d3\_prob5}). This became the paper's central claim for one full revision, including an abstract stating ``answer-only matching is not sufficient'' as the headline result.

\textbf{Correction 3: the regression intercept argument withdrawn.} An earlier reading treated the fitted regression intercept (0.0214, $p = 0.086$) as evidence ``closer to significance'' than the null slope suggested. Reinspection showed 7 of 45 pairs sit at exactly zero state-distance---a direct measurement, not an extrapolation---and all 7 show zero divergence; the fitted intercept was an upward-biased extrapolation from points roughly half a state-unit away. Corrected regression (excess PDI, cluster-robust SEs): intercept 0.0022 ($p = 0.38$) (Sections~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}, \ref{sec:lim-continuous}).

\textbf{Audit 1: confidence-matching follow-up on \texttt{d3\_prob5}.} 4 fresh L1 pairs and 4 fresh L2 pairs from one shared generation run, designed to test whether confidence-matching closes the divergence gap. It does not: one confidence-matched pair still diverges (PDI = 0.169), and the one divergent answer-only pair reverses the original pattern's direction (Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}). Mechanism withdrawn.

\textbf{Audit 2, first attempt: censoring follow-up on \texttt{d1\_prob1}/\texttt{d2\_prob1}, run under the wrong configuration.} Designed to test whether the decisiveness gap is token-budget right-censoring, at doubled token budget. Through an implementation oversight, \texttt{HFBackend} had no way to pin \texttt{top\_p}, so the audit ran under the scale-up's default configuration rather than the primary sample's top-$p=0.95$. Result under the tested configuration: censoring ruled out (all 160 branches terminated naturally), but the audit did not test what it was built to test (Sections~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}, \ref{sec:lim-caution}).

\textbf{Fix: \texttt{top\_p} support added to \texttt{HFBackend}.} The audit was rerun with \texttt{top\_p=0.95} pinned, matching the primary sample's actual configuration, and completed all 4 planned pairs (\texttt{d2\_prob1}'s first attempt was interrupted by a client-side connection drop and rerun separately). Result: 1 of 320 fresh branches empty at 1500 tokens, closed by 3000, censoring confirmed as a real mechanism, but far too rare to explain the original 5.8\% empty rate (Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}).

\textbf{Diagnostic: same-prefix resampling variance in \texttt{d3\_prob5}.} Checking each pair-side's (confidence, entropy, normalized-position) fingerprint showed the 4 ``independent'' original \texttt{d3\_prob5} pairs are built from only 4 distinct prefixes forming 3 distinct pairings, one sampled twice. One prefix's own branch sets, resampled independently with no difference in history whatsoever, produced error rates of 0\%, 10\%, and 30\%---spanning the entire ``effect'' size attributed to path dependence (Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}).

\textbf{Reading 3: pivot back to the null.} With both mechanisms withdrawn and the residual aggregate effect sitting inside the same-prefix sampling noise, the positive finding does not survive. Current framing: a well-supported null that survived two direct stress tests (Section~\ref{sec:lim-stresstests}).
