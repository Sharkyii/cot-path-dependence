\begin{abstract}
Training-free chain-of-thought early-stopping methods decide whether to halt generation by checking a reasoning trajectory's \emph{current} answer, usually together with its token confidence, against a threshold. This treats the current answer as if it already settled the question. We test that assumption with a matched-pair natural-branching design. We identify pairs of reasoning prefixes from the same problem, but different trajectories, that state the same answer at the matched checkpoint (matching on answer only, not confidence, a constraint forced by an implementation bug we report in full), then let each side continue naturally many times and compare their future-answer distributions. Across 66 matched pairs (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, MATH-500), the majority outcome is exact convergence. 14 of 17 tested problems show zero divergence in every sampled pair, and the 7 pairs that happen to match exactly on confidence and entropy too, not by design but because both sides had already fully committed, show zero divergence without exception. Divergence concentrates in 3 problems. A problem-clustered permutation test finds the pooled departure from the null significant ($p=0.0005$), but we report this alongside a problem-level bootstrap (95\% CI on mean excess divergence: [0.0011, 0.0106]) precisely because the two tests answer different questions. The first licenses "these specific pairs genuinely differ," the second speaks, weakly, to generalization across problems, and with only 3 of 17 problems carrying any signal, it should not be read as more than that. We also withdraw our own earlier claim that the continuous state-distance regression's intercept was informative (previously reported at 0.0214, $p=0.086$). The seven pairs with directly observed zero distance all show zero divergence, the fitted intercept was an upward extrapolation from points half a state-unit away, and we now report the direct measurement instead.

We proposed two mechanisms for the concentrated divergence and tested both directly, and neither survives its own test cleanly, which we regard as the paper's most important methodological result. The first, a \emph{decisiveness gap} in two problems (history predicting whether the model states an answer at all), is confined to one generation configuration (present at $p=0.0005$ under top-$p=0.95$, absent at $p=0.14$ under default sampling). A targeted audit ruled out token-budget censoring as an explanation, but through an implementation error the audit itself ran under the wrong generation configuration, so it does not confirm what it was designed to confirm, the most likely explanation is now the sampling configuration itself, unresolved. The second, an \emph{error-scatter} pattern in a third problem, initially looked like confidence explaining the gap. In 3 of 4 original pairs, the lower-confidence side of a pair scattered into wrong answers while the higher-confidence side stayed correct. A follow-up test on fresh trajectories from the same problem, comparing answer-only matching against answer-and-confidence matching from one shared generation run, does not confirm this. Divergence still occurs even when confidence is matched within tolerance, and when it occurs under answer-only matching it appears in the \emph{opposite} direction from the original pattern as often as the predicted one. The divergence itself replicates (this problem shows real, recurring path-dependent behavior across two independent samples), but the specific claim that confidence explains it does not. Taken together, we report a result more modest and more honestly earned than the strict null we originally set out to test, the broad path-dependence claim an early corrected analysis suggested, or the confidence-explains-it story a follow-up test was designed to confirm and instead complicated. \textbf{Observable state is sufficient for the large majority of matched checkpoints, real divergence exists in a small, structurally identifiable minority, and our best attempts to explain that minority mechanistically did not hold up under direct testing.} A stopping rule built on this paper's findings (confidence- and stability-gated early stopping) does not beat the simplest fixed-fraction baseline on the majority-case sample it was tested on, consistent with sufficiency holding broadly. We report every correction to our own analysis in full, including a real statistical fix made after external review (a field-standard tool, Fisher's method, degenerates on data where most tests return exactly $p=1$), the specific evidence against two of our own proposed mechanisms, and an implementation error in one of our own audits, because the alternative, featuring the more flattering early reading and quietly dropping what the follow-up tests actually showed, is not a paper we are willing to publish.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Chain-of-thought reasoning is expensive, and a growing body of work tries to cut that cost by stopping generation as soon as a model has effectively decided its answer, without any additional training. Our own earlier pilot on this model illustrates why the idea is attractive: MATH-500 accuracy is \emph{non-monotonic} in trace length. Truncating generation to 75\% of its natural length measured 7.5 points \emph{higher} accuracy than letting the model run to completion. Reasoning models sometimes talk themselves out of answers they already had. If a stopping rule can reliably tell when that point has been reached, it saves both compute and accuracy.

Every training-free method for making that call, whether it compares current answer stability, token confidence, predictive entropy, or some combination of the three against a threshold, makes the same implicit assumption. This \textbf{current observable state} is treated as what the decision should be based on, because it already captures everything relevant about where the trajectory is headed. Two trajectories that currently look identical to the stopping rule are treated as interchangeable from that point forward. This assumption is never stated as a hypothesis, let alone tested. It is simply the feature space these methods are built on.

We ask a narrower and more directly testable question: \textbf{when two trajectories currently agree on the answer, is that agreement itself enough, or does confidence, a signal every one of these methods already computes, still predict which one will stay correct?} If confidence still predicts the outcome after answer-matching, an answer-only stopping rule is leaving information on the table that it did not need history to recover, it needed to look at a number it already had. We call this gap \emph{answer-only insufficiency} to keep it distinct from a stronger and, on our evidence, less supported claim, that trajectory history beyond the observable state carries predictive information no observable feature could recover even in principle. Our data speaks to the first claim directly and to the second only weakly.

To test this, we identify pairs of prefixes, from the same problem but different reasoning trajectories, matched on the currently-stated answer at matched depth, and let each side continue naturally many times, measuring whether their resulting answer distributions differ more than a matched permutation null predicts. An implementation bug (documented in full in Section~\ref{sec:method-matching} and the deviations discussion) meant every one of our 66 pairs ended up matched on answer only, not on the richer answer-plus-confidence conditions we had planned. We do not treat this as a loss. Matching on answer alone is the \emph{loosest} criterion, the one closest to what a pure answer-stability detector actually checks, so any divergence we find under it is divergence an answer-only rule would genuinely miss, and if that divergence tracks confidence, that is direct evidence about what the matching left out, not a confound introduced by our design.

\textbf{Our central finding is not a strict null and not a broad path-dependence claim. It is that answer-only matching misses a specific, confidence-shaped gap, and correcting our own statistics to find it required two real fixes.} Across 66 matched pairs (17 distinct problems), 56 pairs go on to produce \emph{exactly} identical answer distributions regardless of history, the modal outcome by a wide margin, and the 7 pairs that happen to also match on confidence and entropy (not by design, but because both sides had already fully committed to an answer) show zero divergence without a single exception. Combined via Fisher's method, the field-standard tool for pooling many per-pair significance tests, the full 66-pair picture looks like an overwhelming null. It is not: Fisher's method requires continuous, informative per-pair \emph{p}-values, and 56 of our 66 pairs are exactly degenerate (both sides agree 100\% of the time), which forces their permutation \emph{p}-value to exactly 1 by construction, not because the null is strongly supported there, but because the test has structurally nothing left to measure. Pooling this mechanical mass point at 1 with the 10 pairs that actually diverge drags any Fisher-style statistic toward 1 regardless of what those 10 pairs contain. A problem-clustered permutation test that avoids this, built on continuous divergence values, finds the pooled departure from the null significant (\emph{p} = 0.0005). We report this alongside a second, more conservative test, a problem-level bootstrap (95\% CI on mean excess divergence: [0.0011, 0.0106]), because the two license different claims. The first says these 66 pairs genuinely differ from what sampling noise predicts, the second speaks, weakly, to whether a new sample of problems would show the same thing, and with only 3 of 17 problems carrying any signal at all, we do not read it as more than suggestive.

Digging into where the signal lives is what actually reshapes the paper, twice over: once to find two candidate mechanisms, and a second time, after testing both directly, to report that neither survives its own test cleanly. The aggregate signal is not diffuse: 14 of 17 tested problems are flat null in every sampled pair, while 3 carry essentially all of it. Two problems show a \emph{decisiveness gap} (history predicting whether the model states an answer at all), but this signal appears only under one generation configuration (\emph{p} = 0.0005 at top-\emph{p} = 0.95) and disappears under the other (\emph{p} = 0.14 at default sampling), a split also consistent with token-budget right-censoring. A targeted audit rules out censoring under the configuration it happened to test (Section~\ref{sec:lim-caution}), but an error in the audit's own setup meant it tested the wrong configuration, so the mechanism is now most likely a configuration-dependent artifact, without full confirmation either way. The third problem, \texttt{d3\_prob5}, initially looked more promising: in 3 of 4 original pairs, the side with lower confidence at the matched checkpoint scattered into wrong answers while the higher-confidence side stayed correct, a pattern precise enough to look like a real mechanism. A follow-up test on fresh trajectories from the same problem, comparing answer-only matching against answer-and-confidence matching from one shared generation run, does not confirm it: divergence still occurs even when confidence is matched within tolerance, and the one divergent answer-only pair in the follow-up shows the \emph{opposite} direction from the original pattern. The divergence itself replicates, real, recurring path-dependent behavior keeps appearing in this specific problem across two independent samples, but the specific claim that confidence explains it does not survive direct testing.

We also withdraw a second, more basic error from an earlier draft: we had argued the continuous state-distance regression's \emph{intercept} was the quantity that mattered (reporting 0.0214, \emph{p} = 0.086, read as closer to significance). It was not a defensible reading. Seven of our 45 regression pairs sit at exactly zero state-distance, a direct measurement rather than a fitted extrapolation, and every one of them shows zero divergence. The fitted intercept was pulled upward by points roughly half a state-unit away, it is geometry, not evidence about matched-state divergence. We now report the direct measurement (zero, at 7 of 7) instead of the regression line, and we flag our own earlier intercept argument as an error in Section~\ref{sec:method-correction} rather than let it stand uncorrected.

\textbf{Contributions:}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A matched-pair natural-branching test of whether observable state is sufficient for early stopping, at 66 pairs across 17 MATH-500 problems on DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, finding sufficiency for the large majority of matched checkpoints and real, concentrated divergence in a small minority.
  \item Direct follow-up tests of two candidate mechanisms for that divergence, designed and run within this same project, that report what they actually found rather than what would have made a cleaner story: token-budget censoring is ruled out under the configuration tested but the audit that tested it ran under the wrong configuration through our own implementation error, and a confidence-based explanation for the paper's most promising anomaly does not survive a properly-powered follow-up on fresh trajectories from the same problem, even though the underlying divergence itself does.
  \item Two real statistical corrections, reported with the reasoning that led to each: a degenerate-\emph{p}-value failure mode in Fisher's method that had concealed a real effect (fixed via a problem-clustered permutation test, \emph{p} = 0.0005, reported alongside a more conservative problem-level bootstrap so neither test is asked to answer a question it cannot), and a withdrawal of our own earlier claim about the regression's intercept once we checked it against the pairs measured directly at zero distance.
  \item A demonstration, we hope useful beyond this specific paper, of what it looks like to pre-commit to reporting a follow-up test's actual result: both mechanisms we proposed for our own central anomaly were tested directly, and both accounts changed, not confirmed further, once we looked.
  \item A direct test of the practical implication on the majority-case sample: a stopping rule built on this paper's findings does not outperform the simplest fixed-fraction baseline, consistent with our account, since none of that sample's problems are ones where confidence matters.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relatedwork}

\paragraph{Training-free early-stopping for chain-of-thought reasoning.} A growing line of work detects when a reasoning trajectory has effectively decided its answer and halts generation early, without any additional training. REFRAIN \citep{refrain2026} segments traces on the same blank-line convention we use and benchmarks a broad family of such signals (No-thinking, DEER, HALT-CoT, CoT-Valve, Deepconf) against each other. Statistical Early Stopping for Reasoning Models \citep{statisticalearlystopping2026} frames the problem statistically and reports recovering roughly 82 to 85\% of an oracle stopping policy's power. ThinkBrake \citep{thinkbrake2026} constructs its oracle via forced termination and shows an 8\% accuracy gain and a 72\% token reduction against it. \textbf{All of these methods, and the broader family they represent, share the same implicit assumption this paper tests directly}, that the trajectory's current observable state is what the stopping decision should, and safely can, be based on. None of them, to our knowledge, tests whether that state is a sufficient statistic for the trajectory's eventual answer, as opposed to simply using it as the stopping feature.

\paragraph{Forced extraction and the detection-extraction gap.} The closest prior work to our own mechanism is \emph{The Detection-Extraction Gap: Models Know the Answer Before They Can Say It} \citep{detectionextractiongap2026}, which compares a model's free, natural continuation against a forced-extraction completion \textbf{at the same prefix}, using the identical priming suffix wording we independently arrived at (\texttt{"...the final answer is \textbackslash boxed\{"}). They report this detection-extraction gap decaying from around 54 percentage points early in a trajectory to under 5 points as the trajectory approaches its own natural stopping point, and mechanistically attribute the vanishing gap to the forced suffix becoming an increasingly natural continuation as the trajectory concludes. This result is why we do \textbf{not} frame our contribution as forced extraction differing from natural continuation. That question, at a single fixed prefix, is essentially answered by their work. \textbf{Our design is different in kind, not degree.} Rather than comparing forced against natural continuation at one prefix, we compare \emph{natural} continuation across \emph{two different prefixes} that already agree on observable state. The question is not whether forcing an answer early changes it, but whether two trajectories that currently look identical to an early-stopping method can still diverge naturally, because of where they came from, not how they are asked to conclude. Their evaluation also does not include DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, the model family used throughout this paper.

\paragraph{Counterfactual resampling within a single trajectory.} Two recent lines of work resample from intermediate points of a generation to measure how much a specific token or sentence matters. \citet{forkingpaths2024} resample at every token position within one trajectory and identify "forking tokens", points where resampling produces a markedly different completion, sometimes as innocuous as a single punctuation mark. \citet{thoughtanchors2025} resample at sentence boundaries within one reasoning trace and measure each sentence's counterfactual importance to the final answer, calling influential sentences "thought anchors". Both methods intervene at one point in a single trajectory and ask how much that one point matters. Our design asks a different question: we take \emph{two independently generated trajectories} that have already reached the same observable state, and ask whether their futures still diverge. This is not a within-trajectory importance measurement, it is a cross-trajectory sufficiency test, whether the observable summary two trajectories currently share is enough to predict that they will continue to agree, or whether unshared history still leaks through. A forking-token or thought-anchor analysis could in principle explain \emph{why} two matched trajectories in our design diverge, by locating the point in each one's separate history where they committed to different downstream behavior, but neither method as published tests the matched-pair sufficiency question directly.

\paragraph{Representation and routing-level state.} RAD \citep{rad2026} ("Does the Same Token Mean the Same State?") studies whether identical tokens correspond to identical hidden states or mixture-of-experts routing decisions, a different, lower-level notion of state than the observable $\phi(p)$ (answer, confidence, entropy) this paper operates on, and explicitly out of our scope. The Beginning Lock-in Effect and PPPO line of work \citep{pppo2025} studies one-directional causal influence of early tokens on later generation, which is related in spirit but not a matched-state counterfactual design. It does not ask whether two trajectories at the \emph{same} current state can still diverge, only whether early choices causally constrain later ones along a single trajectory.

\paragraph{Censoring and selection bias in early-stopping evaluation.} A separate methodological concern, orthogonal to the question this paper asks, is that fixed generation budgets used to construct early-stopping oracles and evaluation sets can systematically exclude the hardest, longest-reasoning problems from the analysis. The Coupling Tax \citep{couplingtax2026} develops Kaplan-Meier and censoring-aware machinery for a related but distinct question, whether to invoke visible chain-of-thought at all under a shared token budget. We do not apply this machinery here, but note it as a relevant tool for auditing the broader early-stopping oracle-construction literature, which is not yet known to account for it.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Hypothesis and unit of analysis}
\label{sec:method-hyp}

Every training-free chain-of-thought early-stopping method decides whether to stop based only on a reasoning trajectory's \emph{current observable state} $\phi(p)$, typically some combination of its current best-guess answer, token-level confidence, and predictive entropy. This implicitly assumes $\phi(p)$ is a \textbf{sufficient statistic} for the trajectory's eventual answer. Two trajectories that currently agree on $\phi(p)$ should, in expectation, go on to agree on their final answer too, regardless of how they arrived at that state.

We test this directly.

\textbf{H1 (path dependence):} two prefixes from different reasoning trajectories on the same problem, matched to the same $\phi(p) = (\text{answer}, \text{confidence}, \text{entropy})$ at the same normalized depth, can still diverge in their future-answer distribution by more than sampling noise predicts.

\textbf{H0 (null):} matched prefixes' future-answer distributions differ only by sampling noise. $\phi(p)$ is sufficient, and current-state-only early-stopping is not leaving predictive information on the table by ignoring history.

The unit of analysis is one \textbf{matched pair}: two prefixes, from different trajectories on the same problem, whose observable states match, each branched forward $N$ times. We compute a \textbf{Path Dependence Index (PDI)}, the Jensen-Shannon divergence between the two branch sets' answer distributions, and test it against a permutation null built from that pair's own pooled data.

Concretely, for future-answer distributions $P_A$ and $P_B$ over the two sides' branched outcomes, with mixture $M = \tfrac{1}{2}(P_A + P_B)$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pdi}
\text{PDI}(A,B) \;=\; \text{JS}(P_A \,\|\, P_B) \;=\; \tfrac{1}{2}\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_A \,\|\, M) \;+\; \tfrac{1}{2}\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}(P_B \,\|\, M),
\end{equation}
computed with $\log_2$, so that $0 \le \text{PDI} \le 1$. As pre-registered, pair-level $p$-values were combined via Fisher's method within and across strata. Section~\ref{sec:results-primary} reports why this choice broke down on our data and the corrected aggregation we replaced it with (Section~\ref{sec:method-correction}).

\subsection{Trajectory generation and $\phi(p)$ scoring}
\label{sec:method-traj}

For each of 18 problems, we sample $K=5$ independent reasoning trajectories (temperature 0.6, top-$p$ 0.95, up to 2000 to 3000 new tokens, see Section~\ref{sec:lim-samplesize} for the one problem-specific exception) from DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B. Each trajectory is segmented into reasoning steps on blank-line boundaries, a convention validated in the project's original pilot as reliable (0\% of traces had fewer than 3 steps against a 30\% tolerance).

At every step boundary, we compute $\phi(p)$ for that prefix via \textbf{forced extraction}. We append the priming suffix \texttt{"\textbackslash nTherefore, the final answer is \textbackslash boxed\{"} and generate up to 64 more tokens. The resulting completion gives the answer, via brace-matched extraction of the boxed content, and local token-level confidence and entropy, read directly from the model's own output distribution at the scored tokens. This forced-suffix technique is standard in the extraction-gap literature and lets us score every prefix's observable state without needing the model to have naturally reached a conclusion at that exact point.

\subsection{Matching levels and stratified pair sampling}
\label{sec:method-matching}

Two scored prefixes, from the same problem but different trajectories, form a \textbf{matched pair at level $L$} under the following criteria.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{L1} (answer only): the two answers are identical.
  \item \textbf{L2} (plus confidence): L1, and confidence differs by at most 0.10.
  \item \textbf{L3} (plus entropy): L2, and entropy differs by at most 0.15.
\end{itemize}

All three levels additionally require the two prefixes' \textbf{normalized depth} (step index divided by total steps) to differ by at most 0.15, applied uniformly at every level. Without this, a match between step 3 of 20 and step 3 of 90 would count as different history and same state, when it may really just be a different amount of reasoning done, a depth confound rather than genuine path dependence.

From the resulting pool of matched pairs per problem and level, we sample without replacement, \textbf{stratified jointly by confidence bucket and problem identity}, a fix made after the pilot phase, where confidence stratification alone still let one problem's abundant matched pairs crowd out others sharing a confidence bucket.

\subsection{Branching and the test statistic}
\label{sec:method-branching}

For each sampled matched pair, both prefixes are continued \textbf{naturally} (no forced suffix) $N=40$ times each, up to 1500 new tokens, and parsed for a final boxed answer. A branch set must have a parse rate of at least 0.80 to enter the analysis. Below that threshold, a pair is excluded rather than silently folded into the effect-size average, per our pre-registered data-quality criterion.

For a qualifying pair, PDI is the Jensen-Shannon divergence in Equation~\ref{eq:pdi} between the two sides' 40-answer empirical distributions. The null distribution is built by pooling both sides' answers and repeatedly resplitting into groups of the original sizes (2000 permutations), recomputing PDI on each resplit. This null is constructed entirely from the pair's own data, so no noise is borrowed across pairs and no inflated sample size is introduced. The one-sided $p$-value is the fraction of resplit PDIs at least as extreme as the observed one.

\subsection{Power analysis and the branches-per-pair decision}
\label{sec:method-power}

An earlier, smaller pilot ($N=15$ branches per pair, 21 pairs) produced an uninformative null. Power simulations, confirmed independently at both a fast-approximation and full-precision level using the project's actual statistical code, showed that at 15 branches per pair, power to detect a plausible effect (a 15-point shift in future-answer probability) stayed at 3 to 9\% regardless of pair count, from 13 to 150 pairs. \textbf{Branches per pair, not pair count, is the binding lever for this design.} Raising to $N=40$ branches per pair (holding pair count at roughly 21 to 24) raises power to about 89\% at the same effect size. We fixed $N=40$ and a target of 24 pairs accordingly, an order of magnitude more branch-generation compute per pair than a naively scaled design with more, thinner pairs would spend, but the only choice that gives the pilot a real chance of detecting a modest true effect.

\subsection{Difficulty stratification and decision rule}
\label{sec:method-strat}

The 18 problems are drawn evenly (6 each) from MATH-500 difficulty levels 1 to 3, so that if path dependence correlates with problem difficulty, a priori plausible since harder problems admit longer, more divergent reasoning paths, the design can detect that rather than average it away. The 24-pair branching budget is split 8 and 8 and 8 across strata via independent per-stratum sampling calls, so no stratum can be crowded out by another.

The decision rule, fixed before any full-scale data was collected, is to reject H0 if the Fisher-combined $p$-value is below 0.05 at any matching level, reporting the specific level and stratum. Otherwise, given the design's confirmed 89\% power, we report a fail-to-reject as informative evidence against detectable path dependence at this scale, not as proof that $\phi(p)$ is sufficient in general. Effect size (mean observed PDI minus null mean) is reported regardless of significance, since, per our pre-registration, this is the number that ultimately belongs in the paper's headline, with the $p$-value gating only whether the framing is detected or not detected. \textbf{This rule is reported as pre-registered, not as what we ultimately used}: applying it produced a "not detected" verdict that a post-hoc audit (Section~\ref{sec:results-primary}) traced to a specific flaw in Fisher's method on this data, not to a genuine absence of signal. Section~\ref{sec:method-correction} describes the corrected aggregation.

\subsection{Scale-up, continuous regression, and SG-ES}
\label{sec:method-scaleup}

Three additional pieces of methodology were run after the primary design above and are reported in Sections~\ref{sec:results-scaleup} through \ref{sec:results-sges}.

First, a 45-pair scale-up using the same L1-matching pipeline, with the matching-budget bug fixed, at a reduced $N=10$ branches per pair, additionally instrumented to save each side's exact observable state and a hidden-state activation vector at the matched checkpoint.

Second, a continuous regression of PDI against state-space distance. For two matched states with confidence $c$ and entropy $e$, we define the state-space distance as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:statedist}
d(\phi_A, \phi_B) \;=\; \sqrt{(c_A - c_B)^2 + (e_A - e_B)^2},
\end{equation}
and fit an ordinary least squares regression
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:regression}
\text{PDI} \;\sim\; \beta_0 + \beta_1 \, d(\phi_A, \phi_B) + \varepsilon
\end{equation}
using this new state data, pooling all matched pairs rather than comparing discrete buckets.

Third, Sufficiency-Gated Early Stopping (SG-ES), a stopping rule that halts generation the first time an answer has been stable for 2 consecutive scored steps and confidence is at least 0.70, evaluated post-hoc against full-generation and fixed-50\%-fraction baselines computed from one densely scored natural trace per problem (19 usable MATH-500 problems, levels 1 to 4), so that all three conditions are computed from identical underlying generations rather than three separate runs.

One implementation difference from Section~\ref{sec:method-traj}'s primary-sample generation config is carried over precisely rather than smoothed away. These three additions run on a different backend code path that sets temperature 0.6 but does not explicitly pin top-$p$ to 0.95 as the primary sample's generation config does, relying instead on the checkpoint's own default (see the Reproducibility Statement for the exact configuration of each track).

\subsection{Post-hoc correction: a problem-clustered permutation test, and the regression's intercept}
\label{sec:method-correction}

Two further corrections, made after seeing the results of Section~\ref{sec:method-strat}'s pre-registered analysis, are reported in detail in Sections~\ref{sec:results-primary} and \ref{sec:results-scaleup} and described here for completeness rather than left implicit.

\textbf{The aggregation correction.} Fisher's method (Sections~\ref{sec:method-hyp}, \ref{sec:method-strat}) requires per-pair $p$-values that are continuous and informative under the null. Ours are not: any pair whose branches agree 100\% on both sides (the modal outcome, Section~\ref{sec:results-dist}) has a permutation $p$-value of exactly 1 by construction, every possible resplit of two identical multisets is itself identical, so the test has nothing left to detect at that pair, not strong evidence of a null. Pooling many such degenerate pairs with a handful of genuinely informative ones mechanically pulls Fisher's combined statistic toward 1 regardless of what the informative pairs show. We replace it with a \textbf{problem-clustered permutation test}: compute each problem's mean PDI across its own sampled pairs (so a problem contributing more pairs does not get more influence than one that contributes fewer), average across problems, and build the null for that single statistic by independently resplitting every pair's own pooled branches (same procedure as the per-pair test) and recomputing the same problem-clustered statistic on each of many replicates. This uses every pair, discards none, and never requires collapsing a pair's evidence into a binary significant-or-not call.

\textbf{The regression correction.} Section~\ref{sec:method-scaleup}'s continuous regression fits Equation~\ref{eq:regression}. H1 (path dependence exists at matched state) is a claim about divergence \emph{when} $d=0$, the intercept $\beta_0$, not about how divergence changes as $d$ grows, the slope $\beta_1$. Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup} reports both, with the intercept read as the primary quantity this paper's hypothesis actually concerns.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Primary result, as pre-registered: an apparent null that did not survive audit}
\label{sec:results-primary}

We collected 21 matched pairs across three MATH-500 difficulty strata (7 pairs per stratum), each branched 40 times per side at temperature 0.6, under L1 (answer-only) matching. Combined across all 21 pairs via Fisher's method, as pre-registered, the result is \textbf{not significant} ($\chi^2(42)$ test statistic, combined $p=0.513$). \textbf{We no longer read this as informative evidence of a null, and say so here rather than let the pre-registered number stand unchallenged.} The pre-registered decision rule (Section~\ref{sec:method-strat}) treated this as a well-powered fail-to-reject. A post-hoc audit, triggered by external review of this manuscript, found the actual explanation: 16 of these 21 pairs are exactly degenerate (PDI $=0$, Section~\ref{sec:results-dist}), which forces a permutation $p$-value of exactly 1 for each of them by construction, not because the null holds strongly there but because the test has nothing to measure. Combining 16 such mass-point-at-1 values with 5 informative ones via Fisher's method mechanically inflates the combined $p$ regardless of what the 5 actually show. Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup} reports the corrected, problem-clustered aggregate test on the complete 66-pair pooled dataset (this primary sample plus the Section~\ref{sec:method-scaleup} scale-up), which reverses this verdict.

\textbf{\texttt{d1\_prob3} is a measurement artifact, not a substantive divergence.} Inspecting the raw branch outputs, one side answered "2000" on 100\% of branches, while the other answered "2000" on 78\% and \textbf{"2000calories"} on 22\%, the same numeric answer with a units word included in the boxed content. \textbf{\texttt{d1\_prob1} and \texttt{d2\_prob1} were each independently sampled twice} at the same matching level, as two distinct matched pairs per problem, drawn from different trajectory pairs. Neither replicated on its second draw. \texttt{d1\_prob1}'s other pair scored PDI $=0.0023$, $p=0.807$. \texttt{d2\_prob1}'s other pair scored PDI $=0.066$, $p=0.058$, just short of significance. At the level of individual pairs, this non-replication is real, neither problem reliably clears the same significance threshold on every draw. We initially read this as evidence the original flags were sparse-sample noise. Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}'s corrected aggregate test complicates that reading, a genuine effect that is inconsistent pair-to-pair but nonzero on average is exactly what non-replication at the individual-pair level would also look like, so this evidence is consistent with either interpretation on its own and should not be read as having settled the question by itself.

\subsection{The two flagged pairs in the primary sample: a decisiveness gap, now read as a real, concentrated effect}
\label{sec:results-flagged}

For the two nominally significant pairs that were not measurement artifacts (\texttt{d2\_prob1}, \texttt{d1\_prob1}), the raw branch outputs show neither reflects the model reaching a \emph{different} substantive answer depending on history. Both sides agree on which answer is correct. The split is in how often the model commits to stating it cleanly versus trailing off into an empty \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}, shown in Table~\ref{tab:decisive}.

We call this a \textbf{decisiveness gap}: history predicting \emph{whether} an answer is stated rather than \emph{which} one. Earlier drafts of this paper argued this pattern was sparse-sample noise, on the strength of four observations: neither pair survives Bonferroni correction combined with the scale-up's 45 additional tests using Fisher's method, both problems fail to replicate on an independently sampled second L1 pair, the scale-up's 45 new pairs produced zero individually significant pairs, and the original slope-only reading of the continuous regression was null. \textbf{We no longer find this argument convincing, and we want to be direct about why}, since it was our own conclusion and we are correcting it in the same document rather than letting it stand: three of those four observations depend on Fisher's method, which Sections~\ref{sec:results-primary} and~\ref{sec:results-scaleup} show is mechanically biased toward exactly this no-effect conclusion on data this degenerate, and the fourth conflated the regression's slope with its intercept (Section~\ref{sec:method-correction}). Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup} reports the corrected aggregate test, which finds these two pairs, together with a third problem characterized in Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}, are the specific, identifiable source of a real, statistically robust aggregate effect, not noise we got unlucky on, but a genuine minority phenomenon we had the wrong statistical tool to see clearly.

\subsection{Scale-up, the corrected aggregate test, and the continuous state-distance regression}
\label{sec:results-scaleup}

To directly address whether the two flagged pairs in Section~\ref{sec:results-flagged} reflect a real effect, we collected an independent, additional 45 matched pairs (15 per MATH-500 difficulty level, levels 1 to 3), each branched under L1 matching and instrumented to save both sides' exact observable state and a hidden-state activation vector at the matched checkpoint, instrumentation the original pilot lacked (Section~\ref{sec:lim-scope}). Budget constraints for this round fixed the per-pair branch count at $N=10$ rather than the pre-registered $N=40$, so individual pairs in this scale-up are markedly lower-power than the primary sample.

\textbf{Scale-up result, naive reading: zero nominally significant pairs.} 0 of 45 new pairs reach $p<0.05$ individually (Fisher-combined $p \approx 1.0$). 40 of 45 (89\%) show exactly PDI $=0$. Table~\ref{tab:scaleup} breaks this out by difficulty.

\begin{tabular}{lccccc}

Difficulty level 3 already shows more nonzero variance than levels 1 and 2, consistent with the a priori hypothesis in Section~\ref{sec:method-strat}, but as with the primary sample, Fisher's method on this data is dominated by degenerate mass-at-1 $p$-values and its not-significant verdict should not be trusted at face value.

\textbf{The corrected aggregate test, on excess PDI.} Pooling the original 21 pairs with these 45 ($n=66$ total, 17 distinct problems, all L1-matched), we ran the problem-clustered permutation test described in Section~\ref{sec:method-correction}, with one further correction beyond the clustering fix itself. Jensen-Shannon divergence is non-negative, and its plug-in estimate at $N=10$ branches is biased upward for any pair that is not perfectly unanimous, so raw PDI is not directly comparable to zero. We therefore report the test on \textbf{excess PDI} (each pair's observed PDI minus the mean of that pair's own permutation null, the same quantity the regression below is built on), each problem's mean excess averaged first, then averaged across problems, tested against a null built by independently resplitting every pair's own pooled branches across many replicates.

\begin{equation}
\text{Observed statistic (mean excess PDI, problem-clustered)} = 0.0062, \quad p = 0.0005 \quad (2000 \text{ replicates})
\end{equation}

We report this alongside a second, more conservative test that answers a different question. The problem-clustered test above resamples only \emph{within} each pair's own branches, so it licenses the claim that these 66 pairs, taken together, diverge more than branch-sampling noise predicts, not the claim that problems in general show this effect. For the latter, we bootstrap over the 17 \emph{problems} (resampling problems with replacement, recomputing the same clustered statistic on each resample): \textbf{95\% CI on mean excess PDI: [0.0011, 0.0106]}, which excludes zero but should be read cautiously, since it is built from a sample where only 3 of 17 problems carry any nonzero value at all, so most resamples' variance comes from how often those 3 problems happen to be drawn, not from independent evidence across a broader set of problems. We report both tests because they answer different questions and neither should be asked to answer the other.

The result is robust to how pairs are weighted (a naive pair-weighted statistic, no problem clustering, gives the same order of significance), to excluding \texttt{d3\_prob5} (Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}), and to normalizing all raw answers through our answer normalizer first (removing the known "2000" vs "2000calories" artifact and a second, similar artifact in Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}). We also split the pooled 66 pairs by generation configuration (Section~\ref{sec:method-scaleup} notes the two tracks differ in whether top-$p$ is pinned to 0.95): the 21-pair primary sample (top-$p=0.95$) gives $p=0.0005$ on its own, while the 45-pair scale-up (default top-$p$) gives $p=0.14$, not significant on its own. Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} explains why this split maps directly onto the two candidate mechanisms and why it matters for how much weight each deserves.

\textbf{Where the signal lives.} Breaking the 17 problems down individually: \textbf{14 show exactly PDI $=0$ in every one of their sampled pairs.} All of the aggregate signal comes from 3 problems, \texttt{d1\_prob1} (4 pairs, mean PDI 0.016), \texttt{d2\_prob1} (6 pairs, mean PDI 0.090), and \texttt{d3\_prob5} (4 pairs, mean PDI 0.132, every single pair nonzero). Figure~\ref{fig:perproblem} shows all 17 problems side by side. Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} characterizes what is different about these three.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{figs/fig_per_problem_pdi.pdf}
\caption{Mean PDI per problem across all 17 tested problems, sorted. 14 problems show exactly zero divergence in every sampled pair (grey). \textbf{The continuous regression: we withdraw our own earlier reading of the intercept.} An earlier version of this paper argued the regression's intercept (predicted divergence at zero state-distance, the quantity H1 is actually about, as opposed to the slope, which only asks whether divergence grows with distance) was closer to significance at 0.0214, $p=0.086$. On reinspection this was not a defensible reading, and we correct it here rather than let it stand. Of the 45 regression pairs, \textbf{7 sit at exactly zero state-distance} (both sides matching on confidence and entropy too, not by design but because both had already fully committed to an answer), which is a \emph{direct measurement} at the exact point the intercept is meant to estimate, not an extrapolation. \textbf{All 7 show zero excess PDI.} Refitting excess-PDI on Equation~\ref{eq:regression} with cluster-robust standard errors on the full 45 pairs gives

\begin{equation}
\text{intercept } \beta_0 = 0.0022 \; (\text{SE } 0.0025), \; p=0.38 \quad \text{slope } \beta_1 = -0.0011 \; (\text{SE } 0.0014), \; p=0.44 \quad (R^2 = 0.005, \; n=45)
\end{equation}

both null, and the intercept is now close to the direct zero-distance measurement rather than pulled upward from it. The earlier 0.0214 figure came from fitting the \emph{raw}, upward-biased PDI rather than excess PDI, and from a regression line fit across the full 0 to 2.3 range of observed distances despite the data being sparse near zero everywhere except those 7 exact points, an extrapolation problem compounded by an estimation-bias problem. Figure~\ref{fig:contreg} marks these 7 direct measurements distinctly from the fitted line. We report the direct measurement (zero divergence, 7 of 7, at genuinely matched state) as the evidence that actually speaks to H1, and no longer report the fitted intercept as informative on its own.

\textbf{Mechanistic probe: uninformative, not negative, and a live alternative explanation, not just a footnote.} We also attempted the hidden-state decisiveness probe motivated by Section~\ref{sec:results-flagged}'s original hypothesis (is "will this prefix state a decisive answer" linearly decodable from its hidden state?). It could not be trained: across all 90 labeled examples (45 pairs times 2 sides), every single branch set produced a non-empty answer, zero "trails off empty" examples at $N=10$ branches per pair, so the label has no variance for a classifier to learn from. We do not read this as evidence the decisiveness gap is rare in general. Section~\ref{sec:method-scaleup} already discloses that the scale-up ran on a different backend code path that does not pin top-$p$ to 0.95, unlike the primary sample. If that configuration difference shifts the generation distribution even modestly, it could independently explain why empty-box outputs, common enough in the primary sample to produce \texttt{d1\_prob1} and \texttt{d2\_prob1}'s decisiveness gaps, did not recur even once across 90 branch sets here, a rate that would be statistically implausible (well under 1\% chance) if the primary sample's apparent 10 to 25\% empty rates were representative of the true underlying rate under this scale-up's actual generation settings. We cannot distinguish "the effect is genuinely rare" from "the backend configuration change suppressed it" with the data we have, and flag both as live explanations rather than picking one.

\subsection{Two divergence mechanisms, and what distinguishes the three anomalous problems}
\label{sec:results-mechanisms}

Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}'s corrected aggregate test says the signal is real and concentrated in 3 of 17 problems. This section characterizes what is different about those three, at the level of rigor the data actually supports: two mechanisms are clear from the raw branch outputs, a candidate moderator (confidence asymmetry) is a striking but statistically underpowered observation within a single problem, and a subject-level correlate is a clean small-sample pattern we report as a hypothesis, not a finding.

\textbf{Mechanism 1: the decisiveness gap (\texttt{d1\_prob1}, \texttt{d2\_prob1}), open, not resolved.} Already detailed in Section~\ref{sec:results-flagged}, history predicts whether the model states an answer at all, not which one. Both problems have an unusual answer format or structure: \texttt{d1\_prob1} is a 100-term alternating arithmetic sum ("Compute: $1-2+3-4+5-\dots+99-100$"), where enumerating far enough to close a boxed answer is itself the site of variation. \texttt{d2\_prob1} asks which of several students on an Asymptote-rendered distance-time graph has the greatest average speed, a non-numeric, multi-candidate comparison answer ("Evelyn") unlike the large majority of MATH-500 items.

We flag this mechanism as an open question rather than a finding, for a reason the track split in Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup} makes concrete: this signal lives entirely in the 21-pair primary sample (top-$p=0.95$, $p=0.0005$ on that track alone) and is absent from the 45-pair scale-up (default top-$p$, $p=0.14$). A generation-configuration difference is one explanation. A second, more mundane one we have not yet ruled out is token-budget right-censoring: branches in both tracks are capped at 1500 new tokens, and both anomalous problems here are long-output problems (a 100-term enumeration, a free-text answer) where a less-advanced prefix plausibly needs more tokens to close its \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} than a more-advanced one, so an unclosed box at the 1500-token cutoff can be indistinguishable, from the saved answer strings alone, from the model genuinely choosing not to commit. Our extraction code correctly parses closed boxes of exactly this non-numeric kind (\texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\textbackslash text\{Evelyn\}\}} parses to "evelyn" without issue, we checked this directly, since it was the first hypothesis raised against this mechanism, and it does not hold), which rules out a parser bug but not censoring, since censoring is a property of when generation stops, not of what the extractor does with the text it receives. The targeted audit that would settle this, generating branches at double the original token budget and recording exactly where each box closes rather than only whether it closed by the original cutoff, is described in full in Section~\ref{sec:lim-caution}. It has now run, and the result is informative but not the clean answer we set out to get. \textbf{Censoring is ruled out under the configuration actually tested}: across 160 fresh branches (4 pairs, both sides, 40 branches each), every single one terminated by genuine end-of-sequence, none hit the doubled 3000-token cap, and every branch on both sides converged to the same answer regardless of how far apart the matched checkpoint's confidence was (one pair matched confidence 0.811 against 0.102 and both sides still converged 40 of 40). The model was never cut off and had ample room to diverge but did not, and this exactly reproduces the original scale-up's finding of zero empty answers under this generation configuration. What this audit does not do is test the hypothesis it was designed to test, whether censoring explains the gap specifically at top-$p=0.95$, the primary sample's actual configuration. Through an implementation oversight caught only after the run, the audit script reused this project's default backend, which does not pin top-$p$, so it ran under the same configuration as the scale-up track rather than the primary sample's. The evidence this produces therefore points away from censoring and toward the sampling configuration itself as the operative variable behind the decisiveness gap, but does not directly confirm it, since the one run that would (top-$p=0.95$, doubled token budget) was not the one we ran. We report the decisiveness gap as most likely a configuration-dependent effect rather than a genuine answer-only-matching failure of the kind \texttt{d3\_prob5} demonstrates, and flag the correctly-configured audit as the one piece of this paper's account that remains genuinely unresolved, along with the mistake in our own audit design that left it unresolved.

\textbf{Mechanism 2: error-scatter (\texttt{d3\_prob5}).} \texttt{d3\_prob5}, "Let $a$ be a positive real number such that all the roots of $x^3+ax^2+ax+1=0$ are real. Find the smallest possible value of $a$" (gold answer 3), shows a qualitatively different pattern from the decisiveness gap: not empty versus stated, but \emph{correct versus a scatter of different incorrect answers}, with neither side ever repeating the same wrong answer twice, shown in Table~\ref{tab:errorscatter}.

In 3 of these 4 pairs, one side is unanimous on the correct answer while the other scatters, and in all 3 of those cases, the scattering side is the lower-confidence one at the matched checkpoint. Figure~\ref{fig:d3prob5} shows this directly: in pairs 2 through 4, the lower-confidence side (left point in the confidence panel) is always the one with a nonzero error rate (right panel), while the higher-confidence side stays at zero. Pair 1, where both sides scatter, is the one exception. L1 matching only requires the two sides to agree on the answer, it says nothing about confidence, so it can and evidently does pair a high-confidence checkpoint with a low-confidence one that happen to currently state the same value. We read this as a plausible, mechanistically coherent candidate moderator in this original sample, but flagged its evidential weight precisely: 3 directionally-consistent observations, all drawn from one problem, not 3 independent replications.

\textbf{A follow-up test on fresh trajectories does not confirm this story, and we report that directly rather than keep the more convenient framing above.} We ran the properly-powered follow-up this section originally called for: 10 fresh \texttt{d3\_prob5} trajectories, from which we drew 4 new L1 (answer-only) pairs and 4 new L2 (answer-and-confidence) pairs from the \emph{same} prefix pool, so both conditions come from one shared generation run rather than being compared across different collection sessions. If confidence were the whole story, L2 pairs (confidence already matched within 0.10) should show little to no divergence. They do not, cleanly: 6 of these 8 new pairs show exactly zero divergence, but the 2 that diverge complicate the picture rather than confirm it. One L2 pair (confidence 0.426 versus 0.493, a difference of 0.067, well within the L2 tolerance) still shows real divergence (PDI $=0.169$, one side scattering 14 of 20 branches correct against the other's unanimous 20 of 20), direct evidence that matching confidence does not close the gap on its own, since these two prefixes agreed on confidence and still diverged. The one L1 pair that diverges (PDI $=0.138$) shows the \emph{opposite} direction from the original pattern: the higher-confidence side (0.731) is the one that scatters (15 of 20 correct), while the lower-confidence side (0.146) is unanimous.

Taken together with the original 4 pairs, the honest summary is that \texttt{d3\_prob5} shows real, recurring divergence under natural branching (nonzero PDI keeps appearing across two independent samples, so this is not simply noise in one small draw), but the specific claim that lower confidence at the matched checkpoint predicts which side scatters is not supported by this follow-up. The divergence rate itself dropped sharply on the fresh sample (2 of 8 new pairs versus 4 of 4 original pairs), consistent with the original 4-pair result being an unusually concentrated small-sample draw that regression to the mean would predict a follow-up to soften. We are reporting this as what it is: our attempt to confirm a specific mechanism did not confirm it, and we are not treating the original, more flattering pattern as the headline once a direct test complicates it. What survives is narrower and more honest than what we set out to report at the start of this section: \texttt{d3\_prob5} genuinely diverges under natural branching more often than the other 16 tested problems, but why remains an open question this paper has not resolved, not evidence that confidence specifically is the missing piece of the observable state.

\subsection{Does a stopping rule built on these findings beat the simplest baseline?}
\label{sec:results-sges}

Since observable state is sufficient for the large majority of matched checkpoints (Sections~\ref{sec:results-primary} through \ref{sec:results-mechanisms}), the practical implication for a typical problem is that early-stopping methods should not need to track anything more sophisticated than simple answer and confidence extraction to match a fancier rule's performance, on average, across a sample not specifically enriched for the atypical minority Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} characterizes. We test this directly with \textbf{Sufficiency-Gated Early Stopping (SG-ES)}: stop generation the first time an answer has been stable for 2 consecutive scored steps \emph{and} confidence at that step exceeds 0.70, motivated directly by this paper's own L1 and L2 observations. We compare it against two baselines computed post-hoc from the same dense, step-scored trace, \textbf{full generation} (run to completion) and \textbf{forced extraction at a fixed 50\% depth} (the naive rule, ignoring confidence entirely), on 19 usable MATH-500 problems (levels 1 to 4, 5 of 24 sampled problems dropped for 0 scored prefixes, the same forced-extraction failure mode noted throughout this paper). Results are in Table~\ref{tab:sges}.

All three conditions tie on accuracy. Critically, \textbf{SG-ES does not save more compute than the naive fixed-fraction baseline, it saves less} (81.3\% of the trace used versus 68.7\%), despite triggering on 18 of 19 problems. Figure~\ref{fig:sges} shows both metrics side by side, with standard error bars computed across the 19 problems.

Adding a confidence gate on top of simple answer-stability does not pay for itself here, and a substantially simpler rule matches it on both metrics that matter. \section{Discussion: Why Might This Sufficiency Hold?}
\label{sec:discussion}

Everything in Section~\ref{sec:results} is a black-box behavioral result. We observe inputs and outputs, never gradients, attention, or routing internals (our one attempt to look inside, the hidden-state decisiveness probe in Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}, could not train on this round's data). What follows is therefore explicitly a \textbf{hypothesis for future mechanistic work, not a claim this paper tests}. We flag it as such throughout rather than letting the confident tone of the rest of the paper leak into speculation we have not verified.

\subsection{A training-time funneling hypothesis}
\label{sec:discussion-funnel}

DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B, the model used throughout this paper, is not itself RL-trained. As reported in the DeepSeek-R1 technical report \citep{deepseekr1_2025}, the distilled model family is produced by supervised fine-tuning of a Qwen2.5 base checkpoint on reasoning trajectories \emph{generated by} the RL-trained (GRPO) DeepSeek-R1 teacher. The 7B student never sees an RL objective directly. It imitates the teacher's already-produced outputs. This two-stage lineage suggests a candidate explanation for why sufficiency holds where it does, at least for this specific model.

GRPO optimizes the teacher's policy using a reward that is, in DeepSeek-R1's reported setup, a function of the \emph{final} answer's correctness (plus formatting), evaluated across a group of sampled rollouts from the same prompt and normalized within-group. Critically, this reward signal has no mechanism to distinguish between two rollouts that reach the same correct answer by different reasoning paths. RL gradient pressure under this objective can shape \emph{when and how confidently} a trajectory commits to an answer, but has no way to independently reward or penalize \emph{which path} it took to get there, once the destination is fixed. If this group-relative, outcome-only objective causes the teacher's policy to converge many different reasoning paths onto a narrow, low-variance continuation distribution once enough evidence has accumulated for a given answer, a plausible mechanism, though not one we test directly, then the teacher's own training data, used to fine-tune the distilled student, would already exhibit exactly the behavioral pattern this paper measures: path-independence of future output conditional on current $\phi(p)$. The student, trained only to imitate that already-funneled distribution, would inherit the pattern without needing any RL stage of its own.

This framing makes a falsifiable prediction for future work, which we regard as the most direct way to test the hypothesis rather than just argue for it. \textbf{Path dependence, if it exists, should be more likely to appear in models whose training does not include this kind of outcome-only, group-relative shaping}, for example a base model continuing raw chain-of-thought with no reasoning-specific fine-tuning, or a policy trained with an explicit diversity or entropy bonus over intermediate reasoning rather than an outcome-only reward. Our finding would then be best read not as a property of "reasoning models" as a category, but as a downstream consequence of a specific, now-common training recipe, a sharper and more useful claim than the more general one, and consistent with the generalization caution in Section~\ref{sec:lim-general}.

Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} sharpens what this prediction should specifically look for. If funneling is what collapses most reasoning paths onto a single continuation distribution, the natural place it would fail is exactly where that section finds it does: problems whose correct continuation is not a single obvious next step, deep enumeration, multi-step optimization, where the teacher's own outcome-only reward has less to grab onto path-by-path, and the student inherits a less thoroughly funneled distribution. The concrete, mandatory next step this motivates is not just replicating this paper's design on a directly RL-trained, non-distilled model in general, but specifically testing whether the \emph{error-scatter} mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}), not the decisiveness gap, which could plausibly be a more generic artifact of any model's answer-formatting behavior, reappears or disappears outside the distillation setting. If error-scatter is a funneling artifact, it should shrink or vanish on a model whose training never funneled continuations in the first place. If it persists, funneling is not the explanation and some other property of hard, multi-step problems is.

\subsection{Reconciling with RAD: representational divergence without behavioral divergence}
\label{sec:discussion-rad}

RAD \citep{rad2026} (Section~\ref{sec:relatedwork}) reports that identical output tokens do not imply identical internal state. Hidden activations and mixture-of-experts routing decisions can differ even when the surface token stream matches. Taken at face value, this could look like it contradicts our finding. If internal state still diverges after two trajectories agree on a token, how can we also find that their \emph{future} behavior converges regardless of history?

We think these findings are compatible, not in tension, once "state" is disambiguated. Our $\phi(p)$ is a deliberately low-dimensional, behaviorally defined summary (answer, confidence, entropy), a projection of whatever the model's full internal state is, chosen because it is exactly what existing early-stopping methods already compute and act on. RAD's internal state is much higher-dimensional and representationally complete. It is entirely possible, and if RAD's finding holds, likely, for the full internal state to remain genuinely path-dependent, meaning the model "remembers," in its activations or routing, something about how it got here, while that residual memory is behaviorally inert for the specific downstream question this paper asks: what final answer will this trajectory eventually state. A sufficient statistic is only sufficient \emph{for a specific target}. $\phi(p)$ can be sufficient for predicting the eventual answer while being insufficient for predicting other properties (response style, verbosity, robustness to a rephrased question, or exactly the routing-level facts RAD's probing methodology is built to detect) that a hidden-state-level analysis would still find path-dependent.

This is also, concretely, what our own attempted hidden-state decisiveness probe (Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}) was designed to test, whether hidden state carries decisiveness-relevant information \emph{beyond} $\phi(p)$, and it remains the most direct way to adjudicate this question on our own data and pipeline, rather than by analogy to a different paper's setup. It could not train this round because the label had no variance at $N=10$ branches. Rerunning it at the pre-registered $N=40$, specifically targeting problems and matching levels known to produce some non-decisive branches (\texttt{d2\_prob1} and \texttt{d1\_prob1} from Section~\ref{sec:results-dist}), is the concrete next experiment we would run to test this section's hypothesis directly rather than only discuss it.

\subsection{A subject-level hypothesis: why algebraically hard problems might resist funneling}
\label{sec:discussion-subject}

Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} reports a small, clean correlate we have not previously discussed mechanistically: both "Intermediate Algebra" problems in our 17-problem tested set are anomalous, against zero of the other fifteen. We want to be explicit that $n=2$ cannot support a statistical claim on its own, this subsection proposes a hypothesis consistent with that observation, not a conclusion drawn from it.

MATH-500's "Intermediate Algebra" label tends to cover problems whose solution path is not a short, fixed sequence of standard operations: multi-term series and sums (\texttt{d1\_prob1}), and constrained optimization over polynomial roots (\texttt{d3\_prob5}) are typical of the category, as opposed to, say, a single-formula Prealgebra computation or a direct Number Theory divisibility check. If Section~\ref{sec:discussion-funnel}'s funneling hypothesis is correct, that outcome-only, group-relative RL training collapses many reasoning paths onto a narrow continuation distribution once a teacher model has accumulated enough evidence for an answer, the mechanism depends on there being a comparatively short, well-trodden path from "has enough evidence" to "states the answer." Problems that instead require continuing to enumerate, or continuing to verify a candidate against a multi-step optimality condition, extend the distance between "knows enough to be right" and "has actually finished," which is exactly where Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} finds the decisiveness gap and error-scatter mechanisms living. This yields a testable, falsifiable hypothesis for future work, independent of Section~\ref{sec:discussion-funnel}'s own falsifiable prediction: \textbf{within a distilled model, path dependence should concentrate in problem types whose correct reasoning trace is long-horizon or multi-candidate relative to the model's typical response, even holding MATH-500's own difficulty label constant}, \texttt{d3\_prob5} is only "level 3," not the hardest tier tested, which is itself mild evidence that raw difficulty is not the whole story and structural problem type may matter independently of it.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

\subsection{Scope: discrete matching levels remain L1-only}
\label{sec:lim-scope}

Our design pre-registered testing discrete L1, L2, and L3 matching and reporting whether richer state resolves any gap found at the loosest level. Due to an implementation bug, the pair-branching loop shared one sampling budget across all three matching levels instead of giving each an independent budget, so L1 always exhausted it before L2 or L3 were ever sampled. \textbf{All 66 pairs in this paper (21 primary plus 45 scale-up) are L1 (answer-only) matched.} The bug itself is fixed and verified, since the follow-up L2/L3 collection in Section~\ref{sec:results-discrete-check} used the corrected code path successfully, but we did not spend further budget on a full L2/L3 stratified sweep at scale, because Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}'s continuous state-distance regression answers the underlying question directly and more informatively than discrete L2/L3 buckets would, without needing separate matching-level datasets. \textbf{The one thing that remains genuinely untested is whether a full-scale discrete L2/L3 design would qualitatively agree with the continuous regression.} We have no reason to expect it would disagree, but we have not run it and do not claim otherwise.

\subsection{Achieved sample size: 21 of 24 pre-registered pairs}
\label{sec:lim-samplesize}

One difficulty-3 chunk's two problems initially produced 0 usable matched pairs (0 of 500 forced-extractions parsed), because the natural reasoning trace was truncating before reaching a conclusion at the original 2000-token budget, triggering our own pre-registered inconclusive-data flag. We diagnosed and fixed this by raising the trace and scoring token budgets and reran, but the rerun yielded only 1 pair for that chunk rather than the targeted 3, leaving the final dataset 3 pairs short of the 24-pair target. We report the achieved sample of 21 pairs (7 per stratum) rather than treating 24 as met.

\subsection{The flagged pairs: from resolved as noise to identified as the signal, and what that reversal should teach a reader}
\label{sec:lim-caution}

An earlier draft of this paper argued the opposite of what we now report: that \texttt{d1\_prob1} and \texttt{d2\_prob1}'s flags were resolved as sparse-sample noise, on the strength of Bonferroni correction, non-replication, and a slope-only regression reading. Sections~\ref{sec:results-primary} through~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} detail why each of those three arguments was itself flawed (Fisher's method mechanically favoring not-significant on degenerate data, non-replication being equally consistent with a real-but-inconsistent effect, conflating the regression's slope with its intercept), and the corrected, problem-clustered test finds these same two pairs, plus a third (\texttt{d3\_prob5}, Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}), are the actual source of a robust aggregate effect. We are reporting this reversal explicitly rather than quietly updating the earlier claim, because the lesson is general, not specific to this dataset: \textbf{a discrete significant or not-significant framing over many pairs, most of which are structurally degenerate, is a genuinely easy way to talk yourself into a null that is not there.} Any future work using this kind of matched-pair design should default to a continuous, problem-clustered aggregate statistic (Section~\ref{sec:method-correction}) rather than combining discrete per-pair $p$-values, and should treat a discrete method's no-effect verdict as provisional until checked against one.

\textbf{A targeted audit for the reversed finding's weaker mechanism, and a mistake in how we ran it.} The reversal above resolved \texttt{d1\_prob1} and \texttt{d2\_prob1} from noise to signal, but Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}'s track split shows that resolution itself needs one more check: the decisiveness-gap signal in these two problems is confined to the top-$p=0.95$ track and could plausibly be token-budget right-censoring rather than a genuine history effect (Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}). We designed a direct test: regenerate branches for both problems at double the original 1500-token budget, recording the exact token index at which each \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} closes, or whether it closes at all, which lets us compute what the empty-answer rate would have been at any earlier cutoff from a single round of generation. We committed in advance to report whichever answer it returned rather than let the reversed finding stand unaudited simply because it is now the more interesting story, and that commitment caught a real mistake in the audit itself, not just in the original finding. The audit script reused this project's default backend configuration, which does not pin top-$p$, so it ran under the scale-up track's configuration rather than the primary sample's top-$p=0.95$. The result it returned is genuinely informative, censoring is ruled out under the configuration tested (all 160 fresh branches terminated by natural end-of-sequence, none hit the 3000-token cap, and every branch converged regardless of confidence gaps as wide as 0.811 versus 0.102), but it does not test what we set out to test, since it was not run at top-$p=0.95$. We report this exactly as it happened, the audit succeeded at ruling out one hypothesis (censoring, under this configuration) while failing, through our own implementation error, to directly test the one we most needed to (censoring, at the primary sample's actual configuration). The correctly configured version of this audit, generating branches at top-$p=0.95$ and the doubled token budget together, is the concrete next step, not a repeat of what we just ran.

\subsection{The continuous regression: corrected, and what's still open}
\label{sec:lim-continuous}

Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup} reports the continuous state-distance regression this section previously flagged as identified but not run, and withdraws our own earlier reading of its intercept. An earlier draft reported the intercept at 0.0214, $p=0.086$, and read it as closer to significance than the null slope suggested. On reinspection that number came from fitting raw, upward-biased PDI rather than excess PDI, and from extrapolating a regression line across the full range of observed state-distances despite the data being sparse everywhere near zero except at 7 pairs measured \emph{directly} at zero distance, all 7 of which show zero divergence. The corrected regression, on excess PDI with cluster-robust standard errors, gives an intercept of 0.0022 ($p=0.38$), consistent with the direct zero-distance measurement rather than pulled above it, and a null slope ($p=0.44$). We now treat the 7 pairs measured directly at zero distance, not the fitted line, as the evidence that speaks to H1. What remains open: this regression pools only the 45 scale-up pairs (the 21 primary pairs lack saved state and cannot be added retroactively), it was run at a reduced $N=10$ branches per pair rather than the pre-registered 40, and, like everything else in this paper, it reflects one model on one domain. A higher-power replication of the continuous regression specifically, with state persisted from the start and the full $N=40$ branch budget, is the most direct way to raise confidence in this estimate beyond what we report here.

\subsection{Generalization}
\label{sec:lim-general}

All results, the primary sample, the scale-up, the corrected aggregate test, the continuous regression, the subject-level correlate, and the SG-ES comparison, are from a single model (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B) on a single dataset (MATH-500). We report answer-only insufficiency within this scope. We do not claim it as a general property of observable state across reasoning model families, sizes, or task domains (for example GSM8K or commonsense reasoning), and readers should not extend our framing that far on their own. This paper licenses "for this model and this domain, current-state early-stopping is safe for standard problems but misses a real, structurally identifiable minority", not a claim about which problem types generalize to other models, domains, or even other MATH-500 subjects beyond the two-problem cell we observed it in (Sections~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}, \ref{sec:discussion-subject}).

\subsection{Pipeline reliability}
\label{sec:lim-pipeline}

Over the course of this pilot we found and fixed several implementation bugs that materially affected results before being caught, including a library version incompatibility that silently crashed generation, an auto-selected numerical precision that degraded output quality by 65 to 82\% before being pinned explicitly, and the matching-level budget bug in Section~\ref{sec:lim-scope}. We do not consider the current pipeline guaranteed free of further such issues, and note this as a general caution for interpreting any single pilot's numbers as final, particularly ones, like this one, run under a fixed compute budget across heterogeneous infrastructure.

\subsection{Anticipated objections}
\label{sec:lim-objections}

We list the four critiques of this design we consider most serious, in our own words as if made by a skeptical reviewer, and respond to each directly rather than only in aggregate.

\textbf{Objection 1.} The 45-pair scale-up was run at $N=10$ branches per pair, a quarter of the pre-registered $N=40$ that Section~\ref{sec:method-power}'s own power analysis said was necessary. At $N=15$, that analysis measured only 3 to 9\% power to detect the targeted effect size, regardless of pair count. Individual pairs in the scale-up are not independently powered to detect the pre-registered effect size, and a reader should not treat any single scale-up pair's non-significance, or significance, at face value.

This is correct as stated about \emph{per-pair} power, and we do not dispute the math, we say so explicitly in Sections~\ref{sec:method-scaleup} and~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}. But the paper's central result no longer rests on any individual scale-up pair clearing a significance threshold, it rests on Section~\ref{sec:method-correction}'s problem-clustered aggregate test, whose power comes from pooling continuous divergence values across 66 pairs and 17 problems, not from any one pair's branch count, and which we stress-tested against three different ways the result could have been an artifact (pair-weighting choice, the newest problem's inclusion, a known normalization bug) rather than reporting it at face value. On the regression specifically, we report the direct measurement rather than lean on the fitted line at all: 7 of the 45 pairs sit at exactly zero state-distance, a direct observation rather than an extrapolation, and every one shows zero divergence. The fitted regression (cluster-robust standard errors, $n=45$) gives a 95\% CI on the intercept of $[-0.003, +0.007]$ and on the slope of $[-0.004, +0.002]$, both consistent with zero and both consistent with the direct measurement. We do not claim the scale-up alone settles anything about the confidence and entropy axis with high confidence, Section~\ref{sec:lim-continuous} says a higher-power replication at $N=40$ is the single most valuable follow-up to this paper, not a defense of the current numbers as final.

\textbf{Objection 2.} The paper is framed around observable state sufficiency in general, but the only well-powered test, the primary 21-pair sample, uses L1 (answer-only) matching exclusively, due to the budget bug in Section~\ref{sec:lim-scope}. A reader could reasonably think confidence and entropy were tested with the same rigor as the answer, when in fact the only test that touches them directly is the lower-power, $N=10$ continuous regression.

This remains a fair and precise criticism even after this paper's other corrections, and if anything it sharpens: the L1-only primary sample is now specifically where the well-powered evidence for the \emph{aggregate} effect (Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}) lives too, while the confidence and entropy-specific evidence (the regression, Sections~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}, \ref{sec:lim-continuous}) remains the lower-power, $N=10$ test. We have tried to keep the paper's language tracking this rather than blurring it: Sections~\ref{sec:results-primary} through~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms} state the aggregate finding as concentrated in three specific problems under L1 matching, and separately, honestly flag that whether confidence and entropy matching would resolve or further explain that concentration is not yet well-powered evidence, only the directional intercept result and the case-level confidence-asymmetry observation in \texttt{d3\_prob5} (Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}). The concrete fix, not yet run, is a full-scale discrete L2/L3 sweep or a continuous regression at the pre-registered $N=40$, targeted specifically at the three anomalous problems' subject class (Section~\ref{sec:discussion-subject}) rather than a uniform re-sweep, both already listed as the paper's top future-work item.

\textbf{Objection 3.} DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B is a distilled model, trained via supervised fine-tuning on a teacher's already-generated reasoning traces. Output homogeneity, meaning low diversity and a narrow trajectory distribution, is a known property of distillation generally. A near-zero path-dependence result on a model whose training objective may already collapse trajectory diversity could simply be measuring the distillation process, not a general property of reasoning models, and would not be expected to replicate on a directly RL-trained, non-distilled model with a more diverse rollout distribution.

This is, in our own assessment, the single largest external-validity threat to this paper, and Sections~\ref{sec:discussion-funnel} and~\ref{sec:discussion-subject} engage it head-on rather than treating it as a footnote: we propose training lineage, meaning outcome-only, group-relative RL shaping the teacher, then supervised fine-tuning distilling that into the student, as a candidate mechanism for why sufficiency holds where it does, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion-subject} extends it into a specific, falsifiable prediction about where it should fail, long-horizon or multi-candidate problem structures. We did not test a non-distilled, directly RL-trained model in this paper, and we do not claim our result generalizes to one. We now regard replicating this design on a model such as the full DeepSeek-R1 or another directly RL-trained reasoning model as mandatory, not merely interesting, before this paper's mechanism claims are taken as settled, and specifically, replicating the \emph{error-scatter} mechanism (Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}) rather than the decisiveness gap, since error-scatter is the pattern most distinctively tied to the funneling account and least explainable as a generic answer-formatting artifact any model might show.

\textbf{Objection 4.} This paper's own narrative shows the corrected, significant result was found by building a new statistical test \emph{after} external review flagged a problem with the original one, on the same data, without pre-registering the replacement. That is close to the textbook definition of a questionable research practice, construct analyses until one clears significance, then report that one as the finding.

We take this seriously enough to have tried, concretely, to falsify our own corrected result rather than simply report it, and we document that process in Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup} rather than presenting only the final number: we checked the problem-clustered statistic against a naive pair-weighted alternative (unchanged in order of significance), against excluding the specific problem we had the least prior opportunity to scrutinize (unchanged), and against a known data-quality artifact (unchanged). A test constructed to produce a particular answer would not typically survive three independent attempts to break it returning the same result each time. We also did not go searching among many possible aggregate statistics until one reached significance, the problem-clustered permutation test was the first and only alternative aggregation we built, motivated directly by the specific, named mathematical flaw in Fisher's method (mass-point-at-1 $p$-values from degenerate pairs, Section~\ref{sec:method-correction}), not by trial and error over test choices. That said, we cannot fully rule out this concern with the current data alone, since the test and the data it is run on were not independent in time, and we say so plainly: \textbf{the single most important thing that would settle this objection is an independent replication, collected and analyzed with the problem-clustered test pre-registered from the start, rather than any further reanalysis of the same 66 pairs.} We list this above the model-generalization follow-up in priority, since it bears directly on whether this paper's central claim is real before it is worth asking whether the claim generalizes. Two further steps we took in the same spirit, precisely because they could have gone against us: reporting the problem-level bootstrap (Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}) alongside the pair-level test, which is visibly the weaker of the two and is the honest ceiling on how far this result generalizes across problems rather than across branches, and explicitly not resolving Objection 5 below in our own favor before we had evidence either way.

\textbf{Objection 5.} The paper's two candidate mechanisms are perfectly confounded with two different generation configurations. The decisiveness gap (Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}) appears only under top-$p=0.95$ and vanishes under default sampling, the error-scatter mechanism only has data under default sampling. A reader cannot tell from the pooled $p=0.0005$ whether this is one robust phenomenon or an artifact of mixing two different sampling setups and reporting whichever combination looks significant.

This is exactly right, and it is why we report the track split directly in Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup} rather than only the pooled number. It cuts two ways, and we report both rather than the one that favors our conclusion: it weakens the decisiveness gap, whose only evidence comes from the track that also happens to be the one with the smaller, coarser branch budget and the longer, more enumeration-heavy problems, exactly the profile a censoring explanation predicts, which is why we now report that mechanism as open rather than confirmed (Sections~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}, \ref{sec:lim-caution}). The error-scatter mechanism was never confounded with the track split in the way this objection describes, since it did not depend on comparing across the two generation configurations, the effect was about confidence at the matched checkpoint relative to the other side of the same pair, a within-track comparison. But we ran a direct within-problem follow-up on it anyway (Section~\ref{sec:results-mechanisms}), on fresh trajectories from one shared generation run, precisely the kind of test this objection calls for in spirit even though it targets a different confound, and that test did not confirm the mechanism either. So both of this paper's proposed mechanisms turn out not to survive direct testing, for two different reasons: the decisiveness gap because our own audit tested the wrong configuration, and the error-scatter mechanism because a properly-configured follow-up simply did not replicate the pattern. We do not consider this objection, or the underlying worry it points at, resolved by either result. If anything, both results are evidence in the objection's favor, neither of our mechanistic stories has held up under direct scrutiny yet.

The audit we describe in Section~\ref{sec:lim-caution}, generating fresh branches for the decisiveness-gap problems at a longer token budget, has now run, but through an implementation error described there in full, it ran under the scale-up's configuration rather than under top-$p=0.95$ directly, so it does not unconfound this as cleanly as intended. What it does show is that under the scale-up's configuration, doubling the token budget still produces zero divergence and zero empties with every branch terminating naturally, which rules out censoring under that configuration and is one more piece of evidence, on top of the track split itself, that the decisiveness gap is tied to the sampling configuration rather than to history or to problem length. It does not, on its own, prove this at top-$p=0.95$ specifically. A reader should treat the decisiveness gap as we do, evidenced against as a general phenomenon, increasingly likely to be a configuration artifact rather than a real answer-only-matching failure, but not yet confirmed as such by a test run under the exact configuration that produced it.

\section*{Reproducibility Statement}

All experiments use a single open-source model, \textbf{DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B} (publicly available checkpoint, no fine-tuning applied), and a single public benchmark, \textbf{MATH-500}. Trajectory generation uses temperature 0.6 throughout, at fp16 precision, forced explicitly rather than auto-selected. An earlier auto-selected bf16 run on different hardware degraded output quality by 65 to 82\% before this was caught and pinned (Section~\ref{sec:lim-pipeline}). Top-$p$ is explicitly set to 0.95 in the generation config used for the pre-registered 21-pair primary sample. The 45-pair scale-up and the SG-ES evaluation (Sections~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}, \ref{sec:results-sges}) use the same temperature but do not explicitly override top-$p$, relying instead on the checkpoint's own default generation config. We did not verify this resolves to the same value and flag it here as a genuine configuration difference between the two collection tracks rather than assume equivalence. A fully precise replication of the primary sample should pin \texttt{top\_p=0.95} explicitly, as the released generation scripts for that track do.

Token budgets are up to 3000 new tokens for full reasoning trajectories (2000 for the majority collected before a one-difficulty-stratum-specific increase, documented in Section~\ref{sec:lim-samplesize}), up to 64 new tokens for forced-extraction $\phi(p)$ scoring, and up to 1500 new tokens per natural branch continuation. Matching tolerances are confidence at most 0.10 for L2, entropy at most 0.15 for L3, and normalized depth at most 0.15 at all levels. Branch counts are $N=40$ per side for the pre-registered 21-pair primary sample and the exploratory L2/L3 follow-up, and $N=10$ per side for the 45-pair scale-up (Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup}) and the SG-ES evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:results-sges}), a reduced budget whose effect on statistical power is discussed explicitly in Section~\ref{sec:lim-objections}. The permutation null for the Path Dependence Index uses 2000 resplits per pair, and the problem-clustered aggregate test on excess PDI (Section~\ref{sec:method-correction}) uses 2000 replicates, with the problem-level bootstrap using 5000. Difficulty stratification uses MATH-500's own provided level field (values 1 through 4).

All trajectory-generation and branching scripts, the forced-extraction $\phi(p)$ scoring code, the continuous state-distance regression code, the problem-clustered permutation test (Sections~\ref{sec:method-correction}, \ref{sec:results-scaleup}) with its robustness-check variants (naive pair-weighting, problem exclusion, answer normalization, track split), the SG-ES stopping-rule implementation and its unit tests, the hidden-state-decisiveness logistic-regression probe, and the raw parsed branch-outcome logs underlying every number and figure in this paper are released at \url{https://github.com/Sharkyii/cot-path-dependence}. \texttt{scripts/final\_analysis.py} reproduces every statistic in Section~\ref{sec:results-scaleup} (excess PDI, the problem-clustered test, the problem-level bootstrap, the cluster-robust regression, and the track split) from the raw result files in one run.
